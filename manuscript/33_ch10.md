> The glass of wine that used to help you unwind may be the very thing waking you at 3 a.m.

%% For years, Hannah's evening ritual had been the same: kids in bed, a glass of red, an hour of television. At fifty, she started waking at three every morning, hot and wide awake, heart thudding. She blamed work stress, then the mattress, then the dog. It was her sister-in-law, over dinner one night, who asked casually, "Does it happen on the nights you don't drink?" Hannah didn't know. So she ran an experiment: two weeks, wine only on weekends, with a note on her phone every time she woke up. The pattern was so clear it made her laugh. Weekday nights, she mostly slept through. Fridays and Saturdays, 3 a.m., like an alarm clock.

Hannah's experiment is the heart of this chapter. Food can't make every symptom disappear, but targeted changes can take the edge off many of them. The best way to find out what works for you is to test one change at a time.

Each section below focuses on one common symptom and explains how food and daily habits may help, what may make things worse, and one or two things to try. You'll also find recipes from Part Four that suit each symptom. Three things to keep in mind as you read. First, try one change for two to four weeks and track it in your symptom diary (Appendix A). Second, triggers are personal: what sets off one woman's hot flashes may do nothing to yours. Third, food is a partner to other support, not a replacement for it. If a symptom is seriously affecting your life, talk to your doctor about all your options (Chapter 6).

## Hot Flashes and Night Sweats

Hot flashes start in the brain's temperature control center as estrogen fluctuates and falls (Chapter 2). Diet can't switch them off. The ideas below are comfort measures that many women find helpful, not treatments.

:::evidence Food and hot flashes
The Menopause Society (2023) doesn't recommend diet changes, soy, avoiding triggers, cooling techniques, exercise, yoga or paced breathing as *treatments* for hot flashes, because trials haven't shown a consistent benefit. That doesn't make them useless: many women feel more comfortable with them, and they're good for your health anyway. But if hot flashes are disrupting your life, the treatments in Chapter 6 are the ones proven to work.
:::

### What tends to light the fuse

The most common triggers are:

- alcohol, especially red wine in the evening
- caffeine, in coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate
- spicy food, hot drinks and very hot food
- large meals late in the evening
- sugary foods and refined carbohydrates that send blood sugar swinging

### What can help cool things down

- **Eat regular meals** with protein, fiber and healthy fats to keep blood sugar steady.
- **Keep evening meals moderate,** and finish eating two to three hours before bed if night sweats disturb your sleep.
- **Stay well hydrated,** especially after a hot flash or night sweat, and keep a bottle of cold water with you.
- **Reach for cooling foods and drinks** when you're warm: cucumber, watermelon, berries, salads, chilled soups, yogurt and iced herbal teas.
- **Include soy foods and ground flaxseed if you enjoy them.** Some women notice a modest difference after several weeks, though trials are inconsistent (emerging).
- **Work toward a weight that feels healthy for you.** Some research suggests that weight loss in women with a higher body weight may reduce hot flashes, though not for everyone.

:::try
- Run your own version of Hannah's experiment: keep a trigger diary for two weeks, noting each hot flash and what you ate or drank in the hour before.
- Swap your evening glass of wine for a Tart Cherry Evening Spritz or Cucumber-Mint Cooling Water on at least three nights.
:::

## Sleep

Poor sleep is one of the most exhausting parts of the transition. Night sweats, anxiety, bathroom trips and lighter sleep all play a part, and your evening routine, including what and when you eat, can help.

### Caffeine curfews, evening meals and sleepy foods

- **Set a caffeine curfew.** Caffeine can stay in your system for many hours, so try to have your last caffeinated drink before about 2 p.m. Green and black tea, cola and dark chocolate count too.
- **Limit alcohol.** It may help you fall asleep, but it disrupts sleep later in the night and can trigger night sweats.
- **Time your meals.** Finish your main meal two to three hours before bed, and have a small snack later if you need one. A very large meal and an empty stomach can both keep you awake.
- **Taper fluids in the evening.** Drink well during the day, then ease off in the hour or two before bed to cut down on bathroom trips.

Some foods may give sleep a gentle nudge:

- **Tryptophan-rich foods.** Your body uses this amino acid to make serotonin and melatonin. It's found in turkey, chicken, fish, eggs, dairy, tofu, oats, nuts and seeds, and eating it with some carbohydrate may help it reach the brain.
- **Magnesium-rich foods.** Magnesium plays a role in muscle relaxation and the nervous system. Pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, black beans and whole grains are good sources. Evidence for magnesium supplements and sleep is limited.
- **Tart cherries.** They contain small amounts of natural melatonin, and a few small studies suggest tart cherry juice may modestly improve sleep in some adults (emerging). Choose unsweetened juice and keep the portion small.

**Good bedtime snacks:** a small pot of Greek yogurt with a sprinkle of oats, whole-grain toast with almond butter, a few walnuts with a small banana, a warm mug of milk or soy milk, or a caffeine-free Golden Turmeric Latte.

:::try
- Set a 2 p.m. caffeine curfew for a week and note any change in your sleep.
- Try a small, calming bedtime snack instead of going to bed hungry or overfull.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Baked Cinnamon Pear with Walnuts, Vanilla Chia Pudding Cups, Tart Cherry Evening Spritz, Golden Turmeric Latte, Greek Turkey Meatballs with Tzatziki, Three-Bean Vegetable Chili.

## Mood and Anxiety

Mood changes are very common during the transition (Chapter 5). Food can't replace professional support when you need it, but it can make a meaningful difference to how resilient you feel from day to day.

### Feeding a steadier mind

- **Keep blood sugar steady.** Long gaps between meals, or meals built mostly on refined carbohydrates, can cause dips that show up as irritability, anxiety and low mood. Regular meals with protein are your first line of defense.
- **Eat oily fish twice a week,** or plant omega-3s such as walnuts, chia and ground flaxseed. Omega-3 fats, particularly EPA, are important for brain health, and some studies suggest they may help with depressive symptoms (emerging).
- **Feed your gut.** The gut and brain talk constantly through nerves, hormones and the immune system, a connection known as the **gut-brain axis**, and most of the body's serotonin is made in the gut. A varied, fiber-rich diet with fermented foods supports a healthy microbiome, which may in turn support mood (emerging).
- **Lean Mediterranean.** Several studies link Mediterranean-style eating with a lower risk of depression, and one well-known trial found that improving diet quality helped people with depression feel better (emerging).
- **Cover the basics.** Low levels of folate, B12 and vitamin D have been linked to low mood.
- **Watch alcohol and caffeine.** Alcohol is a depressant, and too much caffeine can heighten anxiety and trigger palpitations.

:::try
- Make sure you never go more than four or five hours without eating during the day this week.
- Add a portion of oily fish, or a tablespoon of ground flaxseed or chia seeds, on three days.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Sheet-Pan Salmon with Broccoli & Sweet Potato, Smoked Salmon & Avocado Rye Toast, Golden Flax & Berry Overnight Oats, Walnut-Date Energy Bites, Kefir Berry Shake.

## Energy and Fatigue

Feeling drained is one of the most common complaints in midlife. Poor sleep, stress, heavy periods and blood-sugar swings all play a part. If you're exhausted all the time, see your doctor to rule out anemia, thyroid problems, low vitamin D or B12, diabetes and depression.

### Fueling a full day

- **Start with protein.** A breakfast with 20 to 30 grams of protein keeps energy steady through the morning and cuts mid-morning cravings, as Keisha discovers in Chapter 9.
- **Check your iron** if your periods are heavy: you may be low before you're anemic. Ask your doctor about a ferritin test, include iron-rich foods such as lean red meat, chicken, fish, lentils, beans, tofu and fortified cereals, and pair plant sources with vitamin C.
- **Think about B12.** Absorption declines with age and with some medicines, and vegans need a reliable source, such as fortified foods or a supplement.
- **Drink enough.** Even mild dehydration makes you tired and foggy.
- **Choose slow-release carbohydrates** like whole grains, beans, lentils and starchy vegetables over white bread, pastries and sweets.
- **Move.** It sounds backward, but regular activity is one of the best energy boosters there is.

:::try
- Anchor your breakfast with protein every day this week.
- Fill a water bottle each morning and aim to finish it by mid-afternoon.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Spinach & Feta Egg Muffins, Besan Chilla with Mint Yogurt, Green Glow Protein Smoothie, Spiced Red Lentil Dal with Spinach, Lentil Bolognese with Whole-Grain Pasta.

## Cravings and Emotional Eating

Many women notice stronger cravings during the transition, especially for sweet or starchy foods in the afternoon and evening.

### Why the 4 p.m. cookie calls your name

Cravings have real causes. Blood sugar dips after meals low in protein and fiber. Poor sleep raises the hunger hormone ghrelin and lowers the fullness hormone leptin. Stress drives appetite through cortisol. Hormonal shifts, including premenstrual changes in perimenopause, play a part. Restriction backfires, because skipping meals or banning foods makes cravings stronger. And emotions such as boredom, loneliness, sadness and exhaustion often show up as hunger for comfort.

### Kinder ways to handle them

- **Eat regular, satisfying meals.** Cravings are much easier to manage when you're not overly hungry.
- **Plan the snack.** If you always want something sweet at 4 p.m., plan for it: an apple with almond butter, a few squares of dark chocolate with almonds, or a yogurt bowl.
- **Pause and ask.** "Am I physically hungry, or is something else going on?" If it's something else, what do you really need? Rest, a break, connection, fresh air?
- **Allow all foods.** Labeling foods "forbidden" makes them more tempting. Enjoy treats slowly, without guilt.
- **Set up your environment.** Keep satisfying snacks easy to reach, and less nourishing ones a little less convenient.
- **Protect your sleep.** It's one of the most powerful craving-busters there is.

If emotional eating feels out of control, or food and eating cause you real distress, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian. You deserve support.

:::try
- Plan your afternoon snack in advance on five days this week.
- When a craving hits, wait ten minutes and drink a glass of water. If you still want the food, enjoy it slowly and without guilt.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Dark Chocolate & Almond Clusters, Apple Slices with Almond Butter & Cinnamon, Berry Greek Yogurt Bark, Banana-Oat Mini Muffins, Spiced Pumpkin Seed Trail Mix.

## Brain Fog

Forgetfulness, trouble concentrating and losing your train of thought are very common in perimenopause (Chapter 5), and they usually improve with time. Food can support your brain along the way:

- **Omega-3 fats** are a major structural part of the brain. Keep up the oily fish, or the walnuts, chia and flaxseed.
- **Steady blood sugar** matters because your brain runs on a constant supply of glucose, and dips make concentration harder.
- **Hydration** matters because even mild dehydration affects attention and memory.
- **Berries and leafy greens** are rich in protective plant compounds. The MIND diet, a blend of the Mediterranean and DASH diets that emphasizes them, has been linked with slower cognitive decline in observational studies (emerging).
- **Sleep** is when your brain clears waste and files away memories, so protecting it is one of the best things you can do for brain fog.

:::try
- Add a handful of berries and a portion of leafy greens to every day this week.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Golden Flax & Berry Overnight Oats, Mediterranean Lentil & Roasted Vegetable Salad, Tuna & White Bean Niçoise Salad, Sheet-Pan Salmon with Broccoli & Sweet Potato, Frozen Yogurt-Dipped Blueberries.

## Bloating and Digestion

Fluctuating hormones affect the gut, so bloating, gas, constipation and a more sensitive stomach are common in the transition, and stress and changes in eating patterns can add to it.

### Calming an unsettled gut

- **Increase fiber gradually.** Fiber is essential, but adding a lot at once causes bloating. Build up over a few weeks and drink plenty of water so fiber can do its job.
- **Include fermented foods.** Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso and tempeh contain live bacteria that may support gut health. Start small if they're new to you.
- **Slow down.** Eating quickly means swallowing more air, so eat slowly and chew well.
- **Walk after meals.** A short walk helps digestion.
- **Notice common triggers.** Fizzy drinks, chewing gum and sugar alcohols (like sorbitol and xylitol in "sugar-free" products) all cause bloating in some people. So can very fatty meals, alcohol, caffeine, and large amounts of beans or cruciferous vegetables if you're not used to them.
- **Get help with suspected sensitivities.** Some people react to fermentable carbohydrates known as FODMAPs (in foods like onion, garlic, wheat, some fruits and legumes). If you suspect this, work with a dietitian rather than cutting out lots of foods on your own.
- **Drink enough** to help prevent constipation.

:::important When to see a doctor
See your doctor if bloating is persistent (most days for three weeks or more). It's especially important if it comes with loss of appetite, feeling full quickly, pain in your abdomen or pelvis, needing to urinate more often, changes in bowel habits, unexplained weight loss or bleeding. It's usually caused by something harmless, but persistent bloating can occasionally be a sign of ovarian or bowel cancer and should always be checked.
:::

:::try
- Add one small serving of a fermented food each day this week.
- Take a 10-minute walk after your evening meal.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Ginger Brown Rice Congee with Soft Egg, Greek Yogurt Power Bowl, Miso-Glazed Tofu Buddha Bowl, Kefir Berry Shake, Golden Turmeric Latte.

## Joint Aches and Stiffness

Estrogen helps calm inflammation and maintain the tissues around your joints, so as it falls, many women notice new aches and stiffness, especially in the morning.

- **Eat the anti-inflammatory way** (Chapter 9): vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, nuts, seeds, olive oil and oily fish.
- **Include omega-3s** regularly.
- **Cook with turmeric and ginger,** which contain natural anti-inflammatory compounds. Research on turmeric (curcumin) supplements for joint pain is promising (emerging), but supplements can interact with some medicines, including blood thinners, so check with your doctor first.
- **Check your vitamin D.** Low levels are common and can add to aches and muscle weakness.
- **Keep moving.** Walking, swimming, cycling, yoga and strength training keep joints mobile and build the muscles that support them.
- **Ease the load on your knees.** Each pound of body weight puts about four pounds of pressure on your knees with each step, so the strength work in Part Six, which builds the muscles that support them, makes a real difference.

- Cook with turmeric, ginger or both at least three times this week.
- Start each morning with the 5-Minute Morning Stretch in Chapter 20.
:::

**Recipes to try:** Turmeric Tofu & Veggie Scramble, Coconut Chickpea & Vegetable Curry, Spiced Red Lentil Dal with Spinach, Baked Cod with Tomatoes, Olives & Capers, Golden Turmeric Latte.

## Your Symptom Cheat Sheet

| Symptom | Foods and habits that may help | Possible triggers to notice |
|---|---|---|
| **Hot flashes and night sweats** | Regular meals with protein, soy foods, flaxseed, cold water, cooling foods | Alcohol, caffeine, spicy food, hot drinks, large late meals |
| **Sleep** | Tryptophan-rich foods, magnesium-rich foods, small bedtime snack, tart cherries | Caffeine after 2 p.m., alcohol, large or spicy late meals |
| **Mood and anxiety** | Steady blood sugar, oily fish, fiber and fermented foods, Mediterranean pattern | Skipped meals, alcohol, too much caffeine |
| **Energy and fatigue** | Protein at breakfast, iron-rich foods with vitamin C, B12, fluids, whole grains | Sugary snacks, skipped meals, dehydration |
| **Cravings** | Regular meals with protein and fiber, planned snacks, good sleep | Restriction, long gaps between meals, poor sleep, stress |
| **Brain fog** | Omega-3s, berries, leafy greens, steady blood sugar, fluids | Dehydration, poor sleep, blood-sugar dips |
| **Bloating and digestion** | Gradual fiber increase, fermented foods, fluids, walking after meals | Fizzy drinks, sugar alcohols, eating quickly, sudden fiber increases |
| **Joint aches** | Anti-inflammatory foods, omega-3s, turmeric, ginger, vitamin D, movement | Highly processed foods, inactivity |

:::summary
- Food can't switch off symptoms, but targeted changes can reduce triggers and support your body. Test one change at a time, as Hannah did.
- For hot flashes, food and trigger-spotting are comfort measures, not treatments; the proven treatments are in Chapter 6.
- For sleep, set a caffeine curfew, limit alcohol and get your meal timing right.
- For mood, energy, cravings and brain fog, steady blood sugar is key: eat regular meals with protein, fiber and healthy fats.
- For bloating, increase fiber gradually and include fermented foods. For joints, lean on anti-inflammatory foods, omega-3s and movement.
:::
