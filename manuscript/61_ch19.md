@chapter 19 | Getting Started Safely
> You don't need to be flexible to start yoga, or strong to start strength training. That's what they're for.

%% You're sixty-seven, and the last time you sat on the floor on purpose was at a grandchild's birthday party, and getting up again was not elegant. Your doctor used the word "osteoporosis" last spring and gave you a pamphlet about exercise, and you nodded, and you put it in a drawer. Today, Dolores, you've taken the pamphlet out again. You've moved the coffee table, found a firm chair and folded a blanket. You're nervous: what if something breaks? So you read the safety notes twice, choose the chair version of every pose, and start with five minutes of breathing. Nothing breaks. Tomorrow, you think, you'll try ten.

This part of the book is a 30-day plan you can do at home, with nothing more than a little floor space, a sturdy chair and a wall. Most days take about 20 to 25 minutes in all: a short yoga sequence followed by a breathing practice or meditation script. Twice a week, a 15-minute strength session takes the place of the yoga sequence, because muscle and bone need a different kind of challenge.

This chapter covers what you need, how to set up, how to adapt every pose to your body, and how to stay safe, especially if you have osteoporosis, joint problems or high blood pressure. Please read it before you begin.

## What Movement Can Do for You Now

Regular physical activity is one of the most powerful things you can do for your health in midlife and beyond, and this plan brings together three kinds of it.

**Yoga** combines movement, breathing and relaxation. Studies in menopausal women suggest it can improve psychological symptoms, such as anxiety and low mood, and overall quality of life. It also builds flexibility, balance and body awareness, which help you move well and avoid falls. It isn't a treatment for hot flashes, but many women find it helps them feel calmer and more at home in their bodies.

**Strength training** is the habit with the strongest evidence for protecting muscle and bone as estrogen falls (Chapter 7). It also improves blood-sugar control, balance and confidence. The two short sessions a week in this plan follow the general guidance of two or more days of muscle-strengthening activity a week.

**Breathing practices and meditation** help calm the nervous system, ease stress and support sleep. The meditation scripts in Chapter 20 can be read slowly to yourself, or recorded on your phone in your own voice and played back.

## What You Need

- A non-slip mat, or a rug or carpet that won't slide
- A sturdy chair without wheels
- A clear wall
- A firm cushion or folded blanket, and a pillow or two
- A belt, scarf or strap (useful for stretches)
- Two cans of food, two full water bottles, or light dumbbells for the strength sessions, when you're ready
- Comfortable clothes you can move in, and a glass of water

## Setting Up Your Space

Choose a spot where you won't be interrupted for 20 to 30 minutes, with enough room to lie down and stretch your arms in every direction. Keep the room comfortably cool, since many women run warm during the transition, and have a window you can open or a fan nearby. If a hot flash starts mid-practice, pause in a restful seated pose, sip cool water and carry on when you're ready. Morning practice is energizing and evening practice can help you wind down: the best time is simply the time you'll actually do it.

## Make Every Pose Yours

Most poses in this plan have easier and harder options. Use them. The right version of a pose is the one you can do with steady breathing and no pain.

- **Use support.** A chair, a wall or a cushion makes many poses more stable and more comfortable. Using them isn't cheating; it's good practice.
- **Move within a comfortable range.** You should feel a stretch or effort, never sharp pain, pinching or numbness.
- **Keep breathing.** If you find yourself holding your breath, ease off a little. Breathe through your nose if you can.
- **Bend your knees** whenever your hamstrings or lower back feel tight.
- **Come out slowly,** especially from lying down, to avoid dizziness.

## Staying Safe

Please check with your doctor or a physical therapist before you start if you have osteoporosis or a previous fracture, a heart condition, high blood pressure, glaucoma, a recent injury or surgery, or any condition that affects your balance. Stop and seek medical advice if you feel chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.

:::important Bone-safe practice if you have osteoporosis or low bone density
- **Avoid rounding your spine under load.** Skip deep forward folds with a rounded back, sit-ups and crunches. Instead, hinge from your hips with a long, straight spine, and use a chair for support.
- **Keep twists gentle.** Twist only within a small, comfortable range, with a long spine, and never force a twist with your arms.
- **Favor spine-lengthening poses,** like Mountain, Sphinx, Locust and Bird-Dog, which strengthen the muscles that support your back.
- **Move slowly and with control.** Avoid fast or jerky movements and anything that risks a fall.
- **Use support for balance poses** by standing near a wall or holding a chair.
- **Every pose and strength move in Chapter 20 has a bone-safe note** where it matters. When in doubt, choose the supported version.
:::

:::try Getting Down to the Floor and Up Again
Many poses start on the floor. Keep a sturdy chair beside your mat, move slowly, and never round your back to get down or up.
**Getting down**
1. Stand facing the chair and hold the seat with both hands.
2. Step one foot back and lower that knee onto a folded blanket, then bring the other knee down.
3. Place your hands on the floor one at a time, so you're on hands and knees.
4. To lie on your back, lower your hips to one side, lean onto that forearm, then roll onto your back.
**Getting up**
1. Bend your knees and roll onto your side. Never sit straight up from lying on your back.
2. Push up onto your hands and knees, then walk your hands to the chair seat.
3. Bring one foot forward, flat on the floor, and press through it to stand, using the chair for support.
4. Pause for a few breaths before you move on, especially if you feel lightheaded.
If getting to the floor isn't safe for you right now, do the standing and chair poses and leave out the floor ones, or ask a physical therapist to show you a method that suits your body.
:::

### Joints and blood pressure

If your knees are sensitive, put a folded blanket under them when kneeling and don't let your knees go past your toes in lunges. If your wrists ache, try poses on your forearms or with your hands on a chair. If you have high blood pressure or glaucoma, avoid holding your breath, keep your head above your heart (skip Downward-Facing Dog and Legs Up the Wall unless your doctor says they're fine, and use the head-supported Child's Pose), and come up from lying down slowly.

### Your pelvic floor

If you have bladder leaks or a feeling of heaviness, breathe out as you make an effort (as you stand up or lift), and avoid bearing down. The pelvic floor exercises in Chapter 7 fit easily into the end of any practice.

## Beyond the Mat: Your Strength Sessions

Yoga builds some strength, especially in the early weeks, but bones and muscles respond best to load that gradually increases. That's why the plan includes two 15-minute strength sessions a week, on set days (Days 3 and 6 in Week 1, then the same rhythm each week).

There are two sessions, **Strength A** and **Strength B**, which use eight simple bodyweight moves described in Chapter 20. Each session follows the same shape: a 3-minute warm-up, two or three rounds of the moves, and a 2-minute cool-down. You'll build gradually over the four weeks:

| Week | Sets and repetitions | How it should feel |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 1 to 2 rounds of 8 repetitions | Comfortable; you're learning the moves |
| Week 2 | 2 rounds of 10 repetitions | The last few repetitions feel like work |
| Week 3 | 3 rounds of 10, moving slowly (3 seconds down) | Challenging but doable with good form |
| Week 4 | 3 rounds of 12, or add light weights | Hard by the last two repetitions |

Move from one exercise straight to the next, and rest for about 60 seconds between rounds. When the clock reaches 15 minutes, finish the move you're on and go to the cool-down: two good rounds still count. If something feels too hard, use the easier option; if it feels easy, use the harder one. And once the 30 days are done, keep going: two sessions a week, for life, is one of the best gifts you can give your future self.

:::summary
- The plan combines yoga, two weekly strength sessions and daily breathing or meditation, all at home with no special equipment.
- Use a chair, wall and cushions freely; the right version of a pose is the one you can do with steady breathing and no pain.
- With osteoporosis, avoid loaded forward rounding and deep twists, hinge from your hips, and favor spine-lengthening poses.
- Check with your doctor first if you have osteoporosis, heart or blood pressure problems, glaucoma or a recent injury.
- Strength sessions build gradually over four weeks, and they're worth keeping for life.
:::
