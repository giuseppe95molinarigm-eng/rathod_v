Alongside nutrition, a handful of everyday habits have strong evidence for helping women feel better through the transition and for protecting long-term health. None of them requires perfection, expensive equipment or hours of free time. They work best when they become part of ordinary life, the way Jo's squats fit between the counter and the fridge.

## Strength Training: The Habit That Pays You Back

If you take one habit from this chapter, make it this one. Strength training (also called resistance training) means working your muscles against resistance: your own body weight, resistance bands, dumbbells, cans of tomatoes or gym machines. It's one of the most effective things you can do in midlife (established). It builds and maintains the muscle that naturally declines with age and helps maintain bone density. It improves blood-sugar control and body composition, sharpens your balance so you're less likely to fall, and lifts mood, sleep and confidence.

### How much is enough?

Guidelines from the World Health Organization recommend muscle-strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on **two or more days a week**. That's it. Two sessions of 15 to 30 minutes can make a real difference.

### Starting from zero

Start where you are. Bodyweight exercises such as standing up from a chair, wall push-ups, glute bridges and step-ups are an excellent beginning, and Part Six includes two 15-minute no-equipment sessions a week. Move slowly and with control, because good form matters more than the number of repetitions. When an exercise starts to feel easy, make it a little harder: add a few repetitions, slow the movement down, or add a light weight or band. By the last two or three repetitions of a set it should feel hard, while you can still keep good form. And leave a day between sessions for the same muscles, because muscles grow stronger while they recover.

:::important Safety first
If you have osteoporosis, a previous fracture, joint problems, high blood pressure or another health condition, check with your doctor or a physical therapist before starting a new program. Many women with osteoporosis can and should strength train, with the right guidance.
:::

## Moving for Your Heart

Aerobic exercise, or cardio, is anything that raises your heart rate and makes you breathe a little harder: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, dancing, hiking or a class. It protects your heart, lowers blood pressure, improves cholesterol, supports a healthy weight and lifts your mood.

Guidelines recommend at least **150 minutes of moderate activity a week** (for example, 30 minutes on five days), or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix. Moderate means you can talk but not sing; vigorous means you can only manage a few words before pausing for breath. It doesn't have to happen all at once. Ten-minute walks after meals count, and walking after you eat has a bonus: it helps flatten blood-sugar spikes.

Beyond formal exercise, all the movement in your day adds up, and long stretches of sitting are linked to poorer health even in people who exercise. Stand up and move for a few minutes every hour, take phone calls on your feet, use the stairs, park a little further away, garden, play with the dog, dance in the kitchen. It all counts.

## Sleep: Cooler, Darker, Calmer

Sleep problems affect a large share of women during the transition, and poor sleep makes almost every other symptom feel worse. Hot flashes and night sweats may need medical treatment, but good sleep habits help everyone.

**Keep it cool.** Aim for a bedroom around 60 to 67°F (15 to 19°C). Choose breathable cotton, linen or bamboo bedding and nightwear, or moisture-wicking fabrics, and layer your bedding so you can throw a layer off or pull one on in seconds. Keep a glass of cold water and a small fan by the bed, and try a cool shower before turning in.

**Set your body clock.** Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time every day, weekends included. Get outside in natural daylight in the morning, even for ten minutes, which helps set your internal clock. Keep naps short, under 30 minutes, and before mid-afternoon.

**Wind down on purpose.** Give the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed to something calming: dim the lights, read, stretch, take a warm (not hot) bath or try one of the evening practices in Part Six. Put screens away an hour before bed, or at least switch them to night mode. A notebook on the nightstand gives worries and to-do lists somewhere to go.

**Mind your evening plate and glass.** Avoid caffeine after early afternoon, limit alcohol (it disrupts sleep and can trigger night sweats), and try not to eat a large or very spicy meal in the two to three hours before bed. If you wake up hungry, a small snack with protein and slow carbohydrates, like a little Greek yogurt with oats or whole-grain toast with nut butter, may help.

If you've been lying awake for around 20 minutes, get up, go to another dimly lit room and do something calm until you feel sleepy, then go back to bed. This helps your brain relearn that bed is for sleeping. If insomnia lasts for weeks, ask your doctor about CBT for insomnia (Chapter 6), the most effective long-term treatment.

:::note Is it sleep apnea?
Sleep apnea, in which breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, becomes much more common after menopause. Signs include loud snoring, gasping or choking at night, waking with a headache or dry mouth, and feeling exhausted despite a full night in bed. It's very treatable, so talk to your doctor if this sounds familiar.
:::

## Stress and the Art of Doing Nothing

Midlife is often one of the busiest stretches of life. Many of us are juggling work, children, aging parents, relationships and our own changing health all at once. Chronic stress keeps cortisol elevated, which can worsen sleep, increase cravings and fat around the middle, and make hot flashes and mood symptoms harder to cope with.

You can't remove stress from your life, but you can build in moments of recovery that let your nervous system reset:

- **Breathe slowly,** with a longer out-breath. It switches on the body's "rest and digest" response within minutes.
- **Meditate for five to ten minutes a day.** It can reduce stress and improve mood and sleep; the 30-day plan in Part Six includes written meditation scripts.
- **Try yoga,** which combines movement, breath and relaxation. Studies show it can improve psychological symptoms and quality of life in menopausal women.
- **Get outside.** Time in nature lowers stress.
- **Say no** to protect your time and energy.
- **Rest without guilt.** It's how your body and mind repair.

## Rethinking What's in Your Glass

### Alcohol

Many women find their relationship with alcohol changes in midlife. The glass of wine that once felt relaxing may now trigger hot flashes, wreck your sleep or leave you anxious the next day. Alcohol also raises the risk of breast cancer, even at low levels (established), contributes to bone loss and falls, adds calories without nutrients while stimulating appetite, and can worsen mood and anxiety.

No amount of alcohol is completely risk-free, but if you choose to drink, keeping it low makes a real difference. The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans no longer set a daily limit; they simply advise drinking less alcohol for better overall health. And in January 2025, the US Surgeon General warned that alcohol raises the risk of at least seven types of cancer, including breast cancer, and that for some cancers the risk may start at one drink a day or less. Try several alcohol-free days a week, alternate alcoholic drinks with water, choose smaller glasses, or explore the growing world of alcohol-free drinks. The Tart Cherry Evening Spritz and the other "soothing sips" in Part Four make lovely stand-ins.

### Caffeine

Coffee and tea can be part of a healthy diet, and both contain beneficial plant compounds. But caffeine can trigger hot flashes in some women, heighten anxiety and palpitations, and interfere with sleep, since it can linger in your system for many hours. Most healthy adults can safely have up to about 400 mg a day (about two to three 12-ounce cups of brewed coffee), but many women in the transition feel better on less. Try having your last caffeinated drink before about 2 p.m., notice whether caffeine affects your hot flashes, sleep or anxiety, and swap some cups for decaf, herbal tea or a Golden Turmeric Latte. Remember that tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate contain caffeine too.

### If you smoke

Smoking is linked to earlier menopause, more severe hot flashes, faster bone loss, heart disease, several cancers and faster skin aging. Stopping at any age brings real benefits, and your heart disease risk starts to fall soon after you quit. You're far more likely to succeed with support, so ask your doctor or pharmacist about stop-smoking medicines, nicotine replacement and free quit services.

## The Muscles Nobody Talks About: Your Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles that supports your bladder, bowel and uterus. Pregnancy, childbirth, aging and lower estrogen can all weaken it. Signs include leaking when you cough, sneeze, laugh or exercise; needing to rush to the bathroom; or a feeling of heaviness in the vagina. These symptoms are common, but you don't have to live with them. Pelvic floor exercises, often called Kegels, work for many women (established):

1. Sit or lie comfortably. Imagine trying to stop yourself passing wind and stop the flow of urine at the same time. Squeeze and lift those muscles inward and upward.
2. **Slow squeezes:** hold for up to 10 seconds, then fully relax for 10 seconds. Repeat up to 10 times.
3. **Quick squeezes:** squeeze and lift quickly, then relax straight away. Repeat up to 10 times.
4. Do a set of both three times a day. Keep breathing normally and avoid clenching your buttocks or thighs.

It can take three months or more to notice a difference. If symptoms persist, ask for a referral to a pelvic floor physical therapist, who can check your technique and offer other treatments. Vaginal estrogen can also help with bladder symptoms.

## People, Purpose and Belonging

Strong social connections are among the most powerful predictors of long-term health and happiness, while loneliness is linked with poorer mental and physical health. Yet midlife can be isolating: children leave home, careers change, relationships shift, and symptoms can make us pull back.

Connection doesn't require a packed social calendar. It might be a regular walk or coffee with a friend, or a menopause support group in person or online. It might be a class, a choir, a book club or volunteering. Or it might simply be an honest conversation with your family, or a call to an old friend you've lost touch with. Purpose matters too. Many women find midlife is the moment to rediscover an old passion or take on a new challenge. What would you love to learn, make or give in the years ahead?

:::try
- Put two 15-minute strength sessions in your calendar this week, just as you would an appointment.
- Take a 10-minute walk after your biggest meal of the day on at least three days.
- Set a 2 p.m. caffeine curfew and notice what happens to your sleep.
:::

## Your Everyday Habits Checklist

[ ] I have strength training planned on two days this week.
[ ] I'm moving for at least 150 minutes a week, in whatever way I enjoy.
[ ] My bedroom is cool, dark and quiet.
[ ] I go to bed and wake up at roughly the same time each day.
[ ] My last caffeine is before early afternoon.
[ ] I have several alcohol-free days each week.
[ ] I'm practicing pelvic floor exercises.
[ ] I have at least one moment of real rest each day.
[ ] I've connected with a friend or loved one this week.

:::summary
- Strength training twice a week is one of the most powerful habits for midlife, protecting muscle, bone, blood sugar and mood.
- Aim for 150 minutes of moderate cardio a week, and move more throughout the day.
- A cool bedroom, a regular schedule, a calming wind-down and less caffeine and alcohol all support better sleep.
- Regular moments of rest, breathing and mindfulness help manage stress.
- Alcohol can trigger hot flashes and raises breast cancer risk. Drinking less is one of the best things you can do for your health.
- Pelvic floor exercises help with bladder leaks for many women, and connection and purpose matter at every age.
:::
