@chapter 7 | Everyday Habits That Make a Difference
> The most powerful medicine is often found in the small things we do every day.

Alongside nutrition, a handful of everyday habits have strong evidence for helping women feel better during the menopause transition and for protecting long-term health. None of them require perfection, expensive equipment or hours of free time. They simply work best when they become part of ordinary life.

This chapter covers movement, sleep, stress, drinks, pelvic floor health and the often-overlooked power of connection.

## Strength Training Made Simple: Protecting Muscle and Bone

If you take just one habit from this chapter, make it this one. Strength training (also called resistance training) means working your muscles against resistance, whether that is your own body weight, resistance bands, dumbbells or gym machines. It is one of the most effective things you can do in midlife (established) because it:

- builds and maintains muscle, which naturally declines with age
- helps maintain bone density and reduces the risk of osteoporosis
- improves blood-sugar control and insulin sensitivity
- supports a healthy metabolism and body composition
- improves balance and reduces the risk of falls
- benefits mood, sleep and self-confidence

### How much do you need?

Health guidelines, including those from the World Health Organization, recommend muscle-strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on **two or more days a week**. That is it. Two sessions of 15 to 30 minutes can make a real difference.

### Getting started

- **Start where you are.** Bodyweight exercises such as sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-ups, glute bridges and step-ups are an excellent beginning. Part Six includes two 15-minute strength sessions a week that need no equipment.
- **Focus on form.** Move slowly and with control. Quality matters more than quantity.
- **Progress gradually.** When an exercise starts to feel easy, make it a little harder: add a few repetitions, slow the movement down, or add a light weight or band. Muscles and bones respond to being challenged.
- **Aim for "challenging but doable."** By the last two or three repetitions of a set, the exercise should feel hard, but you should still be able to keep good form.
- **Rest between sessions.** Muscles grow stronger during recovery, so leave a day between strength sessions for the same muscle groups.

:::note Safety first
If you have osteoporosis, a previous fracture, joint problems, high blood pressure or another health condition, check with your doctor or a physical therapist before starting a new program. They can suggest safe, effective exercises for you. Many women with osteoporosis can and should strength train, with the right guidance.
:::

## Cardio and Everyday Movement for Your Heart

Aerobic exercise, or "cardio," is any activity that gets your heart rate up and makes you breathe a little harder: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, dancing, hiking or an exercise class. It protects your heart, lowers blood pressure, improves cholesterol, supports a healthy weight, and lifts mood.

Guidelines recommend at least **150 minutes of moderate activity a week** (for example, 30 minutes on five days), or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix of both. Moderate means you can talk but not sing; vigorous means you can only say a few words before pausing for breath.

It doesn't have to happen all at once. Ten-minute walks after meals count, and walking after eating has the added bonus of helping to lower blood-sugar spikes.

### The power of simply moving more

Beyond formal exercise, all the movement you do through the day adds up. Sitting for long periods is linked to poorer health, even in people who exercise. Try to:

- stand up and move for a few minutes every hour
- take phone calls while walking
- use stairs instead of elevators where you can
- park a little further away or get off the bus a stop early
- garden, clean, play with children or pets, or dance in the kitchen

Every step counts.

## Sleep Hygiene for Hot Nights and Busy Minds

Sleep problems affect a large share of women during the menopause transition, and poor sleep makes almost every other symptom feel worse. While hot flashes and night sweats may need medical treatment, good sleep habits help everyone.

### Keep cool

- Keep your bedroom cool, ideally around 60 to 67°F (15 to 19°C).
- Use breathable cotton, linen or bamboo bedding and nightwear, or moisture-wicking fabrics.
- Layer your bedding so you can easily throw off or add a layer.
- Keep a glass of cold water and a small fan by your bed.
- Try a cool shower before bed.

### Set your body clock

- Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time every day, including weekends.
- Get outside in natural daylight in the morning, even for ten minutes. This helps set your internal clock.
- Keep naps short (under 30 minutes) and before mid-afternoon.

### Wind down

- Create a calming routine for the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed: dim the lights, read, stretch, take a warm (not hot) bath, or try one of the evening practices in Part Six.
- Put screens away, or at least use night mode, an hour before bed.
- Keep a notebook by the bed and write down worries or to-do lists to get them out of your head.

### Watch what (and when) you eat and drink

- Avoid caffeine after early afternoon (more on this below).
- Limit alcohol, which disrupts sleep and can trigger night sweats.
- Try not to eat a large or very spicy meal within two to three hours of bedtime.
- If you wake hungry, a small snack containing protein and complex carbohydrates, such as a little Greek yogurt with oats or a slice of whole-grain toast with nut butter, may help.

### If you can't sleep

If you have been awake for around 20 minutes, get up, go to another dimly lit room and do something calm until you feel sleepy, then return to bed. This helps your brain relearn that bed is for sleep. If insomnia continues for weeks, ask your doctor about CBT for insomnia (Chapter 6), which is the most effective long-term treatment.

:::note Is it sleep apnea?
Sleep apnea, in which breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, becomes much more common after menopause. Signs include loud snoring, gasping or choking during the night, waking with a headache or dry mouth, and feeling exhausted despite a full night in bed. It is very treatable, so talk to your doctor if this sounds familiar.
:::

## Stress, Cortisol and the Power of Rest

Midlife is often one of the busiest, most demanding times of life. Many women are juggling work, children, aging parents, relationships and their own changing health all at once. Chronic stress keeps the body's stress hormone, cortisol, elevated, which can worsen sleep, increase cravings and abdominal fat storage, and make hot flashes and mood symptoms harder to cope with.

You can't remove all stress from your life, but you can build in regular moments of recovery that help your nervous system reset:

- **Breathing practices.** Slow, deep breathing with a longer out-breath activates the body's "rest and digest" response within minutes.
- **Mindfulness and meditation.** Even five to ten minutes a day can reduce stress and improve mood and sleep. The 30-day plan in Part Six includes guided meditations.
- **Yoga.** Combines movement, breath and relaxation, and has been shown to improve psychological symptoms and quality of life in menopausal women.
- **Time in nature.** Walking in green spaces is linked with lower stress levels.
- **Saying no.** Protecting your time and energy is a health habit too.
- **Rest without guilt.** Rest is not laziness. It is how your body and mind repair.

## Rethinking Your Drinks: Alcohol and Caffeine

### Alcohol

Many women find that their relationship with alcohol changes in midlife. A glass of wine that once felt relaxing may now trigger hot flashes, disrupt sleep or leave you feeling anxious the next day. Alcohol also:

- increases the risk of breast cancer, even at low levels of drinking (established)
- contributes to bone loss and falls
- adds calories without nutrients, and can increase appetite
- can worsen mood and anxiety

There is no amount of alcohol that is completely risk-free, but if you choose to drink, keeping it to a minimum makes a difference. Current US Dietary Guidelines advise that women who drink have no more than one drink a day, and many health organizations now encourage drinking less than that. Try having several alcohol-free days each week, alternating alcoholic drinks with water, choosing smaller glasses, or exploring the growing range of alcohol-free drinks. The Tart Cherry Evening Spritz and other "soothing sips" in Part Four make lovely alternatives.

### Caffeine

Coffee and tea can be part of a healthy diet, and both contain beneficial plant compounds. However, caffeine can trigger hot flashes in some women, worsen anxiety and palpitations, and interfere with sleep, since it can stay in your system for many hours.

- Most healthy adults can safely have up to around 400 mg of caffeine a day (roughly three to four cups of brewed coffee), but many women in the transition feel better with less.
- Try to have your last caffeinated drink before about 2 p.m.
- Notice whether caffeine affects your hot flashes, sleep or anxiety, and adjust accordingly.
- Swap some cups for decaffeinated coffee, herbal teas or a Golden Turmeric Latte.
- Remember that caffeine is also found in tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate.

### Plus: support if you want to stop smoking

Smoking is linked to earlier menopause, more severe hot flashes, faster bone loss, heart disease, several cancers and faster skin aging. Stopping at any age brings real health benefits, and the risks of heart disease begin to fall soon after you quit. You are much more likely to succeed with support. Your doctor or pharmacist can recommend stop-smoking medicines, nicotine replacement and free local or national quit services.

## Pelvic Floor Health

The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles that supports your bladder, bowel and uterus. Pregnancy, childbirth, aging and lower estrogen can all weaken it. Signs of a weak pelvic floor include leaking urine when you cough, sneeze, laugh or exercise; needing to rush to the bathroom; or a feeling of heaviness in the vagina.

These symptoms are common, but they are not something you simply have to live with. Pelvic floor exercises (also called Kegels) are effective for many women (established):

1. Sit or lie comfortably. Imagine you are trying to stop yourself from passing wind and stop the flow of urine at the same time. Squeeze and lift those muscles inward and upward.
2. **Slow squeezes:** hold the squeeze for up to 10 seconds, then fully relax for 10 seconds. Repeat up to 10 times.
3. **Quick squeezes:** squeeze and lift quickly, then relax straight away. Repeat up to 10 times.
4. Aim to do a set of both, three times a day. Keep breathing normally and avoid squeezing your buttocks or thighs.

It can take three months or more to notice a difference. If symptoms persist, ask your doctor for a referral to a pelvic floor physical therapist, who can check that you are doing the exercises correctly and offer other treatments. Vaginal estrogen can also help with bladder symptoms.

## Connection, Community and Purpose

Strong social connections are one of the most powerful predictors of long-term health and happiness. Loneliness, on the other hand, is linked with poorer mental and physical health. Yet midlife can be an isolating time: children leave home, careers change, relationships shift, and symptoms can make us withdraw.

Connection doesn't have to mean a busy social calendar. It might be:

- a regular walk or coffee with a friend
- joining a menopause support group, in person or online
- a class, choir, book club, volunteering or community project
- talking openly with partners, family and colleagues about how you are feeling
- reconnecting with an old friend

A sense of purpose matters too. Many women find midlife is a time to rediscover old passions or take on new challenges. What would you love to learn, create or contribute in the years ahead?

:::try
- Schedule two 15-minute strength sessions into your calendar this week, just as you would an appointment.
- Take a 10-minute walk after your largest meal of the day on at least three days.
- Set a "caffeine curfew" of 2 p.m. and notice whether your sleep changes.
:::

:::summary
- Strength training twice a week is one of the most powerful habits for midlife, protecting muscle, bone, blood sugar and mood.
- Aim for 150 minutes of moderate cardio a week, and move more throughout the day.
- A cool bedroom, a regular schedule, a calming wind-down and limiting caffeine and alcohol all support better sleep.
- Regular moments of rest, breathing and mindfulness help manage stress.
- Alcohol can trigger hot flashes and raises breast cancer risk. Drinking less is one of the best things you can do for your health.
- Pelvic floor exercises help with bladder leaks for many women.
- Connection and purpose are vital for wellbeing at every age.
:::
