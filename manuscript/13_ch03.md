@chapter 3 | Menopause: Reaching the Milestone
> Twelve months without a period marks a turning point. For many women, it is also the start of feeling more like themselves again.

Menopause, the moment when you have gone twelve consecutive months without a period, is a milestone that passes quietly. There is no ceremony, and you only know you have reached it in hindsight. Yet the years around this point are often when symptoms such as hot flashes and sleep problems are at their strongest, and when longer-term changes to metabolism, bones and the heart begin to accelerate.

This chapter looks at what happens around the time of menopause and how to look after yourself through it.

## How Menopause Is Confirmed

For most women over 45, menopause is confirmed simply by looking back: if you have not had a period for twelve months, and there is no other explanation such as pregnancy, certain medications or a medical condition, you have reached menopause.

### Why blood tests are not always needed or reliable

Many women expect a blood test to give them a clear answer. In practice, guidelines such as those from NICE advise that blood tests for FSH are usually *not* needed to diagnose perimenopause or menopause in women over 45 with typical symptoms. The reason is that hormone levels can swing so much during the transition that a single result may be misleading. One month your FSH may look "menopausal"; the next it may be back in the normal range.

Blood tests can be helpful in certain situations, for example:

- if you are under 45 and your doctor suspects early menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency
- if you use hormonal contraception (such as an IUD) that stops your periods, which makes it harder to know where you are in the transition
- if your doctor wants to rule out other causes of your symptoms, such as thyroid disease

If you use hormonal contraception, ask your doctor when and how it is safe to stop, since you can still become pregnant during perimenopause. Contraception is generally advised until one year after your last period if you are over 50, or two years if you are under 50.

## The Symptoms That May Peak Now: Hot Flashes, Sleep and Joints

For many women, the year or two either side of the final period is when vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes and night sweats) are most frequent and intense. Research from the SWAN study found that hot flashes last a median of around seven years in total, and they can continue for more than ten years in some women. That is far longer than the "few months" many women are told to expect, so if yours are lingering, you are in good company.

Sleep often suffers at the same time. Night sweats wake you; anxiety keeps you awake; you may need the bathroom more often during the night. Over weeks and months, poor sleep affects mood, appetite, concentration and resilience. Chapter 7 and Chapter 10 include practical strategies for better nights, and the evening routines in Part Six are designed to help you wind down.

Joint pain and stiffness are also common around menopause, often in the hands, knees, hips and shoulders. Estrogen has anti-inflammatory effects and helps maintain cartilage and the fluid that cushions joints, so its decline can leave joints feeling achy. Gentle, regular movement, strength work and an anti-inflammatory eating pattern (Chapter 9) can all help.

## Desire, Comfort and Connection: Your Sexual Health

This is a topic many women find hard to raise, even with their doctor. Yet changes to sexual health are among the most common effects of menopause, and unlike hot flashes, they often don't improve on their own over time. You deserve to feel comfortable in your body, and there are effective treatments available.

### Libido, vaginal dryness and comfortable intimacy

As estrogen falls, the tissues of the vulva, vagina and bladder become thinner, drier and less elastic. This is called **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)**, and it affects around half of postmenopausal women, possibly more. Symptoms may include:

- vaginal dryness, itching or burning
- discomfort or pain during sex
- needing to urinate more often or more urgently
- more frequent urinary tract infections

Desire can change too. Lower hormones play a part, but so do tiredness, stress, body image, relationship dynamics and simply not feeling like yourself. Low libido is common and nothing to be ashamed of.

What helps:

- **Vaginal moisturizers**, used regularly (two or three times a week), can relieve everyday dryness.
- **Lubricants** used during sex make intimacy more comfortable. Water-based and silicone-based options are widely available.
- **Low-dose vaginal estrogen**, available as a cream, tablet or ring, is very effective for GSM and is absorbed into the bloodstream only in very small amounts. It is described in more detail in Chapter 6.
- **Pelvic floor physical therapy** can help with pain, tension and bladder symptoms.
- **Open conversation** with a partner, and with your doctor, can make an enormous difference.

## The Metabolism Shift and Changes in Body Composition

Many women notice that their body seems to "work differently" around menopause. You may be eating the same foods and moving the same amount, yet your weight creeps up or your shape changes. This is not your imagination, and it is not a lack of willpower.

Several things are happening at once:

- **Muscle naturally declines with age**, and the loss tends to speed up around menopause. Because muscle is metabolically active tissue, less muscle means your body uses slightly less energy at rest.
- **Fat distribution shifts.** With less estrogen, the body tends to store more fat around the abdomen (visceral fat) rather than on the hips and thighs.
- **Insulin sensitivity can decline**, making blood-sugar control a little harder.
- **Sleep and stress** affect hunger hormones, making you more likely to feel hungry and to crave energy-dense foods.

Large studies suggest that the overall slowdown in metabolism with age is smaller than many people think, and that changes in muscle, activity and sleep are bigger factors. That is encouraging, because those are things you can influence. Building and protecting muscle through strength training and enough protein is one of the most effective things you can do for your metabolism, your blood sugar and your long-term health. Chapter 11 covers this in detail, with no crash diets in sight.

## Skin, Hair and Nails: What's Changing and What Helps

Estrogen supports collagen, the protein that gives skin its structure and bounce. In the first five years after menopause, skin can lose up to a third of its collagen. You may notice that your skin is drier, thinner, more sensitive or slower to heal, and that fine lines appear more quickly.

Hair can change too. Some women notice thinning on the scalp, while others find new hair growth on the chin or upper lip, as the balance between estrogen and testosterone shifts. Nails may become more brittle.

What helps:

- **Protect your skin from the sun.** Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the most effective anti-aging step there is.
- **Moisturize generously**, ideally after bathing, with fragrance-free products if your skin is sensitive.
- **Eat enough protein** to give your body the building blocks for skin, hair and nails.
- **Include healthy fats**, such as olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds and oily fish, which support the skin's natural barrier.
- **Eat plenty of colorful fruit and vegetables**, which provide vitamin C (needed to make collagen) and protective antioxidants.
- **Stay hydrated.**
- **Don't smoke**, as smoking significantly speeds up skin aging.
- **Talk to your doctor** about significant hair loss. It can have other causes, including low iron and thyroid problems, which are easy to test for.

## What Your Body Needs Most in This Stage

| Nutrient | Why it matters now | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | Helps protect muscle as it becomes harder to maintain; supports skin, hair and nails | Fish, poultry, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, tofu, tempeh, edamame, beans, lentils |
| **Calcium** | Bone loss speeds up in the years around menopause | Dairy, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, kale, bok choy, almonds |
| **Vitamin D** | Needed to absorb calcium; supports muscle strength | Sunlight, oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; many people need a supplement (ask your doctor) |
| **Fiber** | Supports gut health, steady blood sugar, fullness and healthy cholesterol | Vegetables, fruit, oats, barley, beans, lentils, whole grains, nuts, seeds |
| **Phytoestrogen-rich foods** | May modestly reduce hot flashes for some women | Soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk), ground flaxseed, chickpeas, lentils |

Your calcium needs increase to around 1,200 milligrams a day from age 51, according to the US National Institutes of Health. Food is the best source; if you think you may not be getting enough, speak to your doctor before taking a supplement. Chapter 9 explains how to meet your needs with everyday foods.

:::evidence Soy and hot flashes
**Emerging.** Several reviews suggest that soy isoflavones may modestly reduce how often hot flashes happen, by around a fifth on average, although results vary from study to study. Some women seem to benefit more than others, possibly because of differences in gut bacteria. A recent small trial combining a plant-based diet with half a cup of cooked soybeans a day reported large reductions in hot flashes, but larger studies are still needed. Soy foods are safe and nutritious for most women, so they are worth including if you enjoy them. Chapter 9 looks at soy and breast cancer, a common concern.
:::

:::try
- Choose one calcium-rich food to add to your day, such as a glass of fortified soy milk, a pot of yogurt or a portion of calcium-set tofu.
- Keep a water-based lubricant and a vaginal moisturizer at home, even if you don't need them yet. Discomfort is far easier to address early.
:::

:::summary
- Menopause is confirmed after 12 months without a period. Blood tests are usually not needed if you are over 45 and have typical symptoms.
- Hot flashes often peak around the final period and last around seven years on average, sometimes longer.
- Vaginal dryness, discomfort and bladder changes (GSM) are very common, don't usually improve on their own, and respond well to treatment.
- Changes in weight and shape are driven largely by muscle loss, fat redistribution, sleep and blood-sugar changes, not a lack of willpower.
- Protein, calcium, vitamin D, fiber and phytoestrogen-rich foods are key priorities now.
:::
