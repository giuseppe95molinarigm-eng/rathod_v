%% Carmen was halfway through presenting the quarterly numbers when it started: a slow burn at the base of her neck that rose to her face in seconds. She felt her blouse dampen. Twelve faces looked at her slides while she looked for the nearest window. Later, in the restroom, holding a paper towel under the cold tap, she did the math. Her last period had been the week of her daughter's graduation, more than a year ago. She was fifty-one. She'd assumed that when periods stopped, the rest would stop too. Instead, the hot flashes seemed to be getting stronger. "So this is it," she told her reflection. "This is menopause." Then she pressed the cold towel to her neck and went back in to finish her slides.

Menopause, the day you've gone twelve months in a row without a period, passes quietly. There's no ceremony, and you only know you've reached it looking back. Yet the years around this point are often when hot flashes and sleep problems are at their strongest, and when longer-term changes to metabolism, bones and heart begin to speed up. This chapter is about what happens around the milestone and how to look after yourself through it.

## How You'll Know You're There

For most women over 45, menopause is confirmed simply by looking back: if you haven't had a period for twelve months, and there's no other explanation such as pregnancy, certain medicines or a medical condition, you've reached menopause.

### Why a blood test often can't tell you

Many women expect a blood test to give them a clear answer. In practice, guidelines such as NICE's say that FSH tests usually *aren't* needed to diagnose perimenopause or menopause in women over 45 with typical symptoms. Hormones swing so much during the transition that a single result can be misleading: one month your FSH looks "menopausal," the next it's back in the normal range.

Blood tests do earn their place in some situations: if you're under 45 and your doctor suspects early menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency, if you use hormonal contraception (like an IUD) that stops your periods and hides where you are in the transition, or if your doctor wants to rule out other causes, such as thyroid disease.

If you use hormonal contraception, ask your doctor when and how it's safe to stop, because you can still get pregnant in perimenopause. Contraception is generally advised until a year after your last period if you're over 50, or two years if you're under 50.

## The Symptoms That Can Peak Now

For many women, the year or two either side of the final period is when hot flashes and night sweats are most frequent and intense. The SWAN study found that hot flashes last a median of about seven years in total, and more than ten for some women. That's far longer than the "few months" many of us are told to expect, so if yours are lingering, you're in very good company, and you don't have to simply wait them out. There are effective treatments, which Chapter 6 covers.

Sleep often suffers at the same time. Night sweats wake you, anxiety keeps you up, and you may need the bathroom more often. Over weeks and months, poor sleep affects your mood, appetite, concentration and resilience. Chapters 7 and 10 have practical strategies for better nights, and the evening routines in Part Six are designed to help you wind down.

Joint pain and stiffness are common around menopause too, often in the hands, knees, hips and shoulders. Estrogen has anti-inflammatory effects and helps maintain cartilage and the fluid that cushions your joints, so as it falls, joints can start to ache. Regular movement, strength work and an anti-inflammatory way of eating (Chapter 9) all help.

## Desire, Comfort and Connection

This is the topic many women find hardest to raise, even with a doctor. Yet changes to sexual health are among the most common effects of menopause, and unlike hot flashes, they often don't improve on their own. You deserve to feel comfortable in your body, and there are treatments that work.

As estrogen falls, the tissues of the vulva, vagina and bladder become thinner, drier and less elastic. Doctors call this **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)**, and it affects around half of post-menopausal women, possibly more. It can mean dryness, itching or burning, discomfort or pain during sex, needing to urinate more often or more urgently, and more frequent urinary tract infections.

Desire can change too. Lower hormones are part of it, but so are tiredness, stress, body image, relationship dynamics and simply not feeling like yourself. Low libido is common and nothing to be ashamed of.

What helps? Vaginal moisturizers used two or three times a week can relieve everyday dryness, and lubricants make sex more comfortable; water-based and silicone-based options are easy to find. Low-dose vaginal estrogen, as a cream, tablet or ring, is very effective for GSM and is absorbed into the bloodstream only in tiny amounts (more in Chapter 6). Pelvic floor physical therapy can help with pain, tension and bladder symptoms. And honest conversation, with a partner and with your doctor, can make an enormous difference.

## Your Metabolism Hasn't Broken

Many women feel their body starts "working differently" around menopause. You eat the same foods and move the same amount, yet the scale creeps up or your shape changes.

Here's something that surprises most people: **your metabolism barely changes between the ages of 20 and 60.** A landmark study published in *Science* in 2021, which measured the daily energy use of more than 6,000 people from infancy to old age, found that once you account for body size, metabolic rate stays remarkably steady through adulthood and only begins a slow decline after 60.

So what's going on? Mostly, it's your muscle. Muscle mass naturally declines with age, and the loss tends to speed up around menopause. With less estrogen, your body also tends to store more fat around the abdomen rather than on the hips and thighs. Insulin sensitivity can dip, making blood sugar harder to steady, and poor sleep and stress push up hunger hormones and cravings for quick energy.

That's genuinely good news, because muscle, activity and sleep are things you can influence. Building and protecting muscle, through strength training and enough protein, is one of the most effective things you can do for your metabolism, your blood sugar and your long-term health. ## Skin, Hair and Nails

Estrogen supports collagen, the protein that gives skin its structure and bounce, and skin can lose up to a third of its collagen in the first five years after menopause. You may notice drier, thinner, more sensitive skin that's slower to heal, and fine lines appearing faster. Hair changes too: some women see thinning on the scalp, others new hairs on the chin or upper lip as the balance between estrogen and testosterone shifts. Nails may become more brittle.

Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the most effective anti-aging step there is, and generous moisturizing after bathing, with fragrance-free products if your skin is sensitive, helps a lot. From the inside, enough protein gives your body the building blocks for skin, hair and nails; olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds and oily fish support the skin's natural barrier; and colorful fruit and vegetables provide vitamin C, which your body needs to make collagen. Drink enough water, and if you smoke, stopping slows skin aging dramatically. Significant hair loss is worth raising with your doctor, since low iron and thyroid problems are common, testable causes.

## What to Put on Your Plate Now

| Nutrient | Why it matters now | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | Protects muscle as it gets harder to maintain; supports skin, hair and nails | Fish, poultry, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, tofu, tempeh, edamame, beans, lentils |
| **Calcium** | Bone loss speeds up in the years around menopause | Dairy, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, kale, bok choy, almonds |
| **Vitamin D** | Needed to absorb calcium; supports muscle strength | Sunlight, oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; many people need a supplement (ask your doctor) |
| **Fiber** | Supports gut health, steady blood sugar, fullness and healthy cholesterol | Vegetables, fruit, oats, barley, beans, lentils, whole grains, nuts, seeds |
| **Phytoestrogen-rich foods** | May modestly reduce hot flashes for some women | Tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, ground flaxseed, chickpeas, lentils |

Your calcium needs rise to about 1,200 milligrams a day from age 51, according to the US National Institutes of Health. Food is the best source, and Chapter 9 shows how to get there with everyday meals. If you think you're falling short, talk to your doctor before taking a supplement.

:::evidence Soy and hot flashes
**Emerging.** Several reviews suggest soy isoflavones may modestly reduce how often hot flashes happen, by around a fifth on average, although results vary between studies. Some women seem to benefit more than others, possibly because of differences in gut bacteria. A recent small trial that combined a plant-based diet with half a cup of cooked soybeans a day reported large reductions in hot flashes, but bigger studies are needed. Soy foods are safe and nutritious for most women, so they're worth including if you enjoy them. Chapter 9 tackles the question of soy and breast cancer.
:::

:::try
- Add one calcium-rich food to your day, such as a glass of fortified soy milk, a pot of yogurt or a portion of calcium-set tofu.
- Keep a water-based lubricant and a vaginal moisturizer at home, even if you don't need them yet. Discomfort is far easier to deal with early.
:::
