%% Keisha's breakfast hadn't changed in fifteen years: a slice of toast with butter, eaten standing up, and a large coffee in the car. It had always worked. Lately it didn't. By 10:30 she was shaky, short-tempered and raiding the vending machine at the hospital where she managed the front desk, and by evening she was too tired to cook. She was forty-eight. When a friend suggested she "eat more protein," Keisha laughed. "I'm not a bodybuilder." But she tried it for a week: Greek yogurt with berries and walnuts, or two eggs and a slice of whole-grain toast, eaten sitting down. On Thursday, at 10:30, she realized she hadn't thought about the vending machine once.

There's no single "menopause diet," and you won't find a list of forbidden foods here. Instead you'll find one method that works for any cuisine, budget or dietary preference, built around the nutrients your body needs most right now. It's three words long: **Anchor, Add, Adapt.**

## If Food Has Felt Complicated

Many of us arrive at midlife carrying decades of diet messages: low-fat, low-carb, points, shakes, cleanses, "clean eating." For some, food has become tangled up with guilt, anxiety or a sense of failure. If that's you, please know this chapter isn't another set of rules to get right or wrong.

Anchor, Add, Adapt is a guide, not a test. Some meals will fit it beautifully; others won't, and that's completely fine. A slice of birthday cake, takeout pizza with friends or a bowl of your grandmother's pasta are part of a full and happy life. What matters is the overall pattern of your eating over weeks and months, not any single meal. If you have a history of disordered eating, or thinking about food causes you real distress, a doctor or registered dietitian can offer personalized support.

## The Method in Three Words

**Anchor** every meal with protein: around 20 to 30 grams, roughly a palm-and-a-half of fish, chicken, tofu or eggs, or a generous bowl of Greek yogurt or lentils.

**Add** plants: vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, nuts and seeds, filling about half your plate and aiming for 30 different plants across the week.

**Adapt** to where you are: your stage of the transition, your symptoms this week, your appetite and your life.

You can picture it on a plate. Fill half with vegetables (and some fruit), a quarter with protein, and a quarter with fiber-rich carbohydrates such as whole grains, beans or starchy vegetables. Finish with a little healthy fat, like a drizzle of olive oil, a sprinkle of seeds or a few slices of avocado. The plate is the picture; Anchor, Add, Adapt is the way of thinking that makes it work at breakfast, at your desk and on a Tuesday night when the fridge looks empty.

| Plate section | Why it matters in menopause | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Plants (half the plate)** | Fiber, antioxidants, potassium and folate for your gut, heart and weight | Spinach, kale, broccoli, peppers, tomatoes, berries, carrots, bok choy |
| **Protein (a quarter)** | Protects muscle and bone, steadies blood sugar, keeps you full | Salmon, chicken, eggs, tofu, lentils, Greek yogurt, edamame |
| **Fiber-rich carbs (a quarter)** | Steady energy, fiber, B vitamins | Oats, quinoa, brown rice, sweet potato, whole-grain bread, beans |
| **Healthy fats (a little)** | Heart and brain health; help you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K | Olive oil, avocado, walnuts, almonds, flaxseed, chia seeds |

## Anchor: Why Protein Comes First

Think about the breakfast you grabbed this morning. Toast and coffee? That's around 5 grams of protein, at exactly the stage of life when your muscles need it most. If you change one thing on your plate, make it this.

Protein provides the building blocks for muscle, bone, skin, hair, enzymes and hormones, and it's the most filling of all the nutrients, keeping you satisfied for longer and your blood sugar steady. As estrogen falls, it gets harder for your body to hold on to muscle. Enough protein, especially alongside strength training, is one of the most effective ways to protect your muscle, your metabolism and your independence for decades to come.

Try this for yourself: tally up your usual breakfast for a few days using the table below. Many women are surprised by how little protein the first meal of the day contains.

### How much, and when

The official minimum (the RDA) for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. But the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans now advise **1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day**. For a woman weighing 150 pounds (68 kg), that's roughly 82 to 109 grams a day. (If you have kidney disease, check with your doctor before increasing protein.)

Timing matters as much as the total. Most of us eat a little protein at breakfast, a bit more at lunch and most of it at dinner. Muscles seem to respond best when it's spread more evenly, with around **20 to 30 grams at each meal**; for many women over 50, 25 to 30 grams is a good target. That's what anchoring means. And it adds up: three meals of 25 to 30 grams plus a protein-rich snack of about 10 grams gets most women close to the daily range.

## Add: Plants, Fiber and Your Gut

Once your protein is in place, add plants. Fiber is the part of plant foods your body can't fully digest, and that's exactly why it's so valuable. It travels to your large intestine and feeds the trillions of beneficial bacteria that make up your **gut microbiome**. Along the way it keeps you regular, slows the absorption of carbohydrates so blood sugar stays steadier, helps lower cholesterol (especially the soluble fiber in oats, barley, beans and lentils), and keeps you full.

Most adults eat far less fiber than recommended. Aim for **25 grams or more a day**, which is easiest when every meal includes vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, nuts or seeds. If you're not used to much fiber, increase it gradually over a few weeks and drink plenty of water to avoid bloating.

### Thirty plants a week

A target that's far more fun than counting grams: **30 different plant foods a week.** Researchers from the American Gut Project found that people who ate 30 or more different plants a week had more diverse gut bacteria than those who ate ten or fewer. It's an association rather than proof of cause, but it's a fun, practical goal. Every vegetable, fruit, whole grain, bean, lentil, nut, seed, herb and spice counts, so a stir-fry with five vegetables, garlic, ginger and sesame seeds gets you eight in one go.

### Meet the estrobolome

This next part is fascinating. Some of the bacteria in your gut make enzymes that help decide how much estrogen is recycled back into your bloodstream and how much leaves the body. Scientists call this collection of bacteria the **estrobolome** (emerging). A healthy, diverse microbiome seems to support healthy estrogen balance, and an imbalanced one may disrupt it.

The research is young, and nobody can yet tell you exactly how to "optimize" your estrobolome. But we do know the microbiome thrives on a varied, fiber-rich diet with plenty of plants, and that fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut and miso can increase microbial diversity. Gut bacteria also turn soy isoflavones into a compound called equol, which may explain why some women benefit more from soy than others.

## The Fats That Help

Fat is essential. It helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, supports brain and hormone health, and makes food satisfying and delicious. The key is choosing mostly unsaturated fats:

- **Monounsaturated fats:** extra virgin olive oil, avocado, almonds, cashews and peanuts.
- **Omega-3 fats:** oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout, herring, anchovies), walnuts, ground flaxseed, chia and hemp seeds, and soy foods.

Omega-3s support heart health and are an important part of the brain. If you eat fish, aim for **oily fish twice a week.** If you don't, include plant sources every day. The body converts only part of the plant form (ALA) into the forms found in fish (EPA and DHA). Vegetarians and vegans may want to ask a doctor or dietitian about an algae-based omega-3 supplement.

It's wise to go easy on **saturated fat,** found mainly in fatty and processed meats, butter, cream, cheese, coconut oil, pastries and fried foods. It can raise LDL cholesterol, which tends to climb after menopause. You don't need to avoid it completely; just make unsaturated fats your everyday choice.

## Soy, Flax and the Phytoestrogen Question

Phytoestrogens are natural plant compounds with a structure similar to estrogen, and they can bind weakly to estrogen receptors in the body. There are two main types. **Isoflavones** are found mainly in soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, miso and soybeans) and in smaller amounts in chickpeas and other legumes. **Lignans** are found in ground flaxseed, sesame seeds, whole grains and some fruits and vegetables.

They're much weaker than your own estrogen and don't act like hormone therapy. Research on hot flashes is mixed (emerging). Some women notice a modest improvement after several weeks of eating soy foods regularly, others notice nothing, and The Menopause Society doesn't recommend soy as a hot-flash treatment because results are inconsistent. Enjoy soy foods for their protein, fiber and calcium, and see any effect on hot flashes as a possible bonus.

### Soy and breast cancer: clearing up the confusion

Many women have been told to avoid soy because of fears about breast cancer. The research now tells a clear story (established). In large population studies, eating soy foods is **not linked to a higher risk of breast cancer**, and in Asian populations with high lifelong soy intakes it's associated with a lower risk. For women who've had breast cancer, eating soy foods doesn't appear to increase the risk of recurrence and may be linked to a lower one. The American Institute for Cancer Research and the American Cancer Society both consider moderate amounts of soy foods safe for women, including breast cancer survivors.

That research is about whole soy foods. Concentrated isoflavone supplements are a different matter, and women with a history of hormone-sensitive cancer should talk to their oncology team before taking them.

## The Small Nutrients With Big Jobs

If your meals follow Anchor, Add, Adapt, you'll meet most of these needs from food. Vitamin D is the main exception for many people, especially in fall and winter or if you're rarely outdoors, and women following a vegan diet need a vitamin B12 supplement. Ask your doctor whether a blood test or supplement is right for you.

## Adapt: Make It Yours

The third word is what makes the method last. Adapting means the framework bends to your life instead of the other way around.

**Adapt to your stage.** In perimenopause, lean on iron-rich foods if your periods are heavy. Around menopause, add soy and flax and keep calcium high. After menopause, nudge your protein up and keep up the oily fish (Chapter 8 has the details).

**Adapt to your symptoms.** Hot flashes, poor sleep, cravings, bloating and joint pain each respond to specific tweaks, which Chapter 10 walks through one by one.

**Adapt to your appetite and your day.** Very active, or trying to build muscle? You may need more fiber-rich carbohydrates and protein. Smaller appetite? Make every bite count. Eating out, traveling or cooking for a family? Look for the anchor and the plants, and let the rest go.

**Adapt to your culture and your kitchen.** Dal and rice, tacos with beans and slaw, congee with egg and greens, a Mediterranean grain bowl: they all fit. There's no single way to eat for menopause.

### An anti-inflammatory way of eating

Low-grade, long-term inflammation is linked with heart disease, type 2 diabetes, joint pain and other conditions that become more common after menopause. An anti-inflammatory way of eating isn't a special diet; it looks a lot like the Mediterranean diet, one of the best-researched eating patterns in the world. Enjoy often: vegetables, fruit (especially berries), beans and lentils, whole grains, nuts and seeds, extra virgin olive oil, oily fish, herbs and spices like turmeric, ginger, garlic and cinnamon, and green tea. Enjoy less often: ultra-processed food, processed meat, refined grains, sugary drinks and sweets, fried food and alcohol. Every time you add a vegetable, swap a sugary drink for water or reach for olive oil, you're moving your overall pattern in the right direction.

## Portions Without a Scale

Weighing and measuring food can feel tedious and, for some of us, stressful. Your hand is a quick, portable guide that's naturally scaled to your body.

| Food group | Your hand | Roughly equals | Per meal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | 1 to 1.5 palms (thickness and size of your palm) | 3 to 5 oz (85 to 140 g) meat, fish or tofu | 1 to 1.5 portions |
| **Plants** | 2 fists | About 2 cups | 2 or more |
| **Fiber-rich carbs** | 1 cupped hand | About 1/2 to 2/3 cup cooked grains or starchy vegetables | 1 to 2, depending on hunger and activity |
| **Healthy fats** | 1 thumb | About 1 tbsp oil, nut butter, nuts or seeds | 1 to 2 |

These are starting points, not limits. Pay attention to your hunger and fullness, and aim to finish meals comfortably satisfied, not stuffed.
