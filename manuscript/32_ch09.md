@chapter 9 | The Menopause Plate: Your Everyday Framework
> You don't need to count every calorie or follow complicated rules. You need a simple picture you can bring to every meal.

There is no single "menopause diet," and you won't find a list of forbidden foods in this chapter. Instead, you will find a flexible framework that works for any cuisine, budget or dietary preference, and that brings together the nutrients your body needs most during this time of life.

We call it the **Menopause Plate**.

## If Food Has Felt Complicated: A Gentle Note Before We Begin

Many women arrive at midlife with decades of diet messages behind them: low-fat, low-carb, points, shakes, cleanses, "clean eating." For some, food has become tangled up with guilt, anxiety or a sense of failure. If that is you, please know that this chapter is not another set of rules to get right or wrong.

The Menopause Plate is a guide, not a test. Some meals will fit it beautifully; others won't, and that's completely fine. A slice of birthday cake, a takeout pizza with friends or a bowl of your grandmother's pasta is part of a full and happy life. What matters is the overall pattern of your eating over weeks and months, not any single meal.

If you have a history of disordered eating, or find that thinking about food causes you significant distress, please consider talking to your doctor or a registered dietitian, who can offer personalized support.

## The Plate Method

Picture a standard dinner plate, about 9 to 10 inches (23 to 25 cm) across, and fill it like this:

### Half vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter fiber-rich carbs, plus healthy fats

- **Half the plate: vegetables (and some fruit).** Choose a variety of colors: leafy greens, broccoli, peppers, carrots, tomatoes, cauliflower, zucchini, mushrooms, beets and more. Fresh, frozen and canned all count. Vegetables provide fiber, vitamins, minerals and protective plant compounds, and they fill you up for very few calories.
- **A quarter of the plate: protein.** Fish, seafood, chicken, turkey, eggs, lean meat, tofu, tempeh, edamame, beans, lentils, Greek yogurt or cottage cheese.
- **A quarter of the plate: fiber-rich carbohydrates.** Whole grains (such as brown rice, quinoa, oats, barley, whole-wheat pasta or bread), starchy vegetables (potatoes with their skins, sweet potatoes, corn, squash), or beans and lentils, which count as both protein and carbohydrate.
- **Plus healthy fats.** A drizzle of extra virgin olive oil, a sprinkle of nuts or seeds, a few slices of avocado, or the natural fats in oily fish.

Add flavor freely with herbs, spices, garlic, ginger, citrus, vinegar and chili. Drink water or another unsweetened drink alongside.

| Plate section | Why it matters in menopause | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Vegetables and fruit (half)** | Fiber, antioxidants, potassium, folate; supports gut, heart and weight | Spinach, kale, broccoli, peppers, tomatoes, berries, carrots, bok choy |
| **Protein (a quarter)** | Protects muscle and bone, steadies blood sugar, keeps you full | Salmon, chicken, eggs, tofu, lentils, Greek yogurt, edamame |
| **Fiber-rich carbs (a quarter)** | Steady energy, fiber, B vitamins | Oats, quinoa, brown rice, sweet potato, whole-grain bread, beans |
| **Healthy fats (a little)** | Heart and brain health, absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K | Olive oil, avocado, walnuts, almonds, flaxseed, chia seeds |

This plate works for breakfast and lunch too, although the proportions will look a little different. A breakfast might be Greek yogurt (protein) with berries (fruit), oats (fiber-rich carbs) and walnuts (healthy fats). A packed lunch might be a grain bowl, a hearty soup with beans, or a wrap filled with protein and salad.

## Protein: Your Midlife Power Nutrient

If there is one nutrient to prioritize during and after the menopause transition, it is protein. Protein provides the building blocks for muscle, bone, skin, hair, enzymes and hormones. It is also the most filling of all the nutrients, helping you feel satisfied for longer and keeping blood sugar steady.

As estrogen declines, it becomes harder for the body to maintain muscle. Getting enough protein, especially alongside strength training, is one of the most effective ways to protect your muscle, metabolism and long-term independence.

### How much, how to spread it across the day, plant and animal sources

The official minimum recommendation for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. Many experts in healthy aging now suggest that adults over 50 may benefit from more, around **1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram per day**, and possibly higher for those who are very active. For a woman weighing 150 pounds (68 kg), that is roughly 68 to 82 grams of protein a day. (If you have kidney disease, check with your doctor before increasing your protein intake.)

Just as important is **how you spread it out.** Many of us eat little protein at breakfast, a little more at lunch and most of it at dinner. Research suggests that muscles respond best when protein is spread more evenly, with around **25 to 30 grams per meal**.

## Fiber and the Gut-Hormone Connection

Fiber is the part of plant foods that your body can't fully digest. Instead, it travels to your large intestine, where it feeds the trillions of beneficial bacteria that make up your **gut microbiome**. Fiber supports:

- regular bowel movements and less constipation
- steady blood sugar, by slowing the absorption of carbohydrates
- healthy cholesterol levels (especially soluble fiber from oats, barley, beans and lentils)
- fullness and appetite control
- a diverse, healthy gut microbiome

Most adults eat far less fiber than recommended. Aim for **25 grams or more a day**. The easiest way to get there is to include vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, lentils, nuts or seeds at every meal. If you are not used to much fiber, increase it gradually over a few weeks and drink plenty of water, to avoid bloating.

A helpful goal is to aim for **30 different plant foods a week**. Research from the American Gut Project found that people who ate this many different plants had more diverse gut bacteria. Every fruit, vegetable, whole grain, bean, lentil, nut, seed, herb and spice counts, so it is easier than it sounds.

### Meet the estrobolome: how your gut helps process estrogen

Here is something fascinating. Some of the bacteria in your gut produce enzymes that help regulate how much estrogen is recycled back into the bloodstream or removed from the body. Scientists call this collection of bacteria the **estrobolome** (emerging). A healthy, diverse microbiome appears to support healthy estrogen balance, while an imbalanced one may affect it.

Research in this area is still at an early stage, and we can't yet say exactly how to "optimize" the estrobolome. But we do know that the gut microbiome thrives on a varied, fiber-rich diet with plenty of plants, and that fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut and miso can increase microbial diversity. Gut bacteria also play a role in converting soy isoflavones into a compound called equol, which may explain why some women benefit more from soy than others.

## Healthy Fats and Omega-3s

Fat is essential. It helps your body absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, supports brain and hormone health, and makes food satisfying and delicious. The key is choosing mostly unsaturated fats:

- **Monounsaturated fats:** extra virgin olive oil, avocado, almonds, cashews, peanuts and their butters.
- **Omega-3 fats:** oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout, herring, anchovies), walnuts, ground flaxseed, chia seeds, hemp seeds and soy foods.

Omega-3 fats support heart health and are an important part of the brain. Aim to eat **oily fish twice a week** if you eat fish. If you don't, include plant sources of omega-3 daily. The plant form (ALA) is converted only partly into the forms found in fish (EPA and DHA), so vegetarians and vegans may wish to talk to a doctor or dietitian about an algae-based omega-3 supplement.

It is wise to limit **saturated fats**, found mainly in fatty and processed meats, butter, cream, cheese, coconut oil, pastries and fried foods, since they can raise LDL cholesterol, which tends to increase after menopause. You don't need to avoid them completely: just make unsaturated fats your everyday choice.

## Phytoestrogens: Soy, Flax and Legumes, What the Evidence Says

Phytoestrogens are natural plant compounds with a structure similar to estrogen. They can bind weakly to estrogen receptors in the body. The two main types are:

- **Isoflavones**, found mainly in soy foods such as tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, miso and soybeans, and in smaller amounts in chickpeas and other legumes.
- **Lignans**, found in flaxseed (especially ground flaxseed), sesame seeds, whole grains and some fruits and vegetables.

Phytoestrogens are much weaker than the body's own estrogen and do not work like hormone therapy. Research on hot flashes is promising but mixed (emerging): some women notice a modest improvement after several weeks of eating soy foods regularly, while others notice no difference. Two to three servings of soy foods a day is a typical amount used in studies, for example a cup of soy milk, half a cup of edamame and a portion of tofu.

### Soy and breast cancer: clearing up the confusion

Many women have been told to avoid soy because of fears about breast cancer. Here is what the research now shows (established):

- In large population studies, eating soy foods is **not linked to a higher risk of breast cancer**. In Asian populations with high lifelong soy intakes, soy appears to be associated with a lower risk.
- For women who have had breast cancer, studies suggest that eating soy foods does not increase the risk of recurrence, and may be linked to a lower risk.
- Organizations such as the American Institute for Cancer Research and the American Cancer Society consider moderate amounts of soy foods safe for women, including breast cancer survivors.

The research applies to whole soy foods, such as tofu, edamame, tempeh and soy milk. Concentrated soy isoflavone supplements are a different matter, and women with a history of hormone-sensitive cancer should discuss them with their oncology team before using them.

## Key Micronutrients

Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts but play big roles. These are the ones most important during and after the menopause transition.

### Calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, iron, B vitamins: food-sources table

| Nutrient | Daily need (women) | Why it matters | Top food sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Calcium** | 1,000 mg (to age 50); 1,200 mg (51+) | Bone strength, muscle and nerve function | Milk, yogurt, cheese, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, canned sardines or salmon with bones, kale, bok choy, almonds |
| **Vitamin D** | 600 IU (to age 70); 800 IU (71+) | Calcium absorption, bone and muscle health | Sunlight on skin, oily fish, egg yolks, fortified milks and cereals; supplements often advised |
| **Magnesium** | Around 320 mg | Muscle relaxation, sleep, blood sugar, bone | Pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, spinach, black beans, edamame, whole grains, dark chocolate |
| **Iron** | 18 mg (while periods continue); 8 mg (after menopause) | Energy, oxygen transport, concentration | Lean red meat, poultry, fish, lentils, beans, tofu, fortified cereals, spinach (with vitamin C) |
| **Vitamin B12** | 2.4 mcg | Energy, nerves, red blood cells | Fish, meat, eggs, dairy, fortified plant milks, nutritional yeast |
| **Folate** | 400 mcg | Cell repair, mood, heart health | Leafy greens, beans, lentils, asparagus, avocado, fortified grains |
| **Potassium** | Around 2,600 mg | Healthy blood pressure | Beans, lentils, potatoes, sweet potatoes, bananas, spinach, yogurt |

*Daily needs are based on US National Institutes of Health recommendations for adult women and may differ slightly by country.*

If you eat a varied diet based on the Menopause Plate, you will meet most of your needs from food. Vitamin D is the main exception for many people, especially in autumn and winter or if you spend little time outdoors. Women who follow a vegan diet should take a vitamin B12 supplement. Ask your doctor whether a blood test or supplement is right for you.

## Anti-Inflammatory Eating Made Simple

Inflammation is a normal part of the body's defense system. But low-grade, long-term inflammation is linked with heart disease, type 2 diabetes, joint pain and other conditions that become more common after menopause.

An anti-inflammatory eating pattern is not a special diet. It is, in fact, very similar to the Mediterranean diet, one of the best-researched eating patterns in the world:

- **Eat more:** vegetables, fruit (especially berries), beans and lentils, whole grains, nuts and seeds, extra virgin olive oil, oily fish, herbs and spices (such as turmeric, ginger, garlic and cinnamon), green tea.
- **Eat less:** ultra-processed foods, processed meats, refined grains, sugary drinks and sweets, fried foods, and excess alcohol.

You don't have to be perfect. Every time you add a vegetable, swap a sugary drink for water or choose olive oil, you move your overall pattern in the right direction.

## Portion Guidance with the Hand Method (No Scales Needed)

Measuring and weighing food can feel time-consuming and, for some women, stressful. Your hand offers a quick, portable guide that is naturally scaled to your body size.

| Food group | Your hand | Roughly equals | Per meal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | 1 to 1.5 palms (thickness and size of your palm) | 3 to 5 oz (85 to 140 g) meat, fish or tofu | 1 to 1.5 portions |
| **Vegetables** | 2 fists | About 2 cups | 2 or more |
| **Fiber-rich carbs** | 1 cupped hand | About 1/2 to 2/3 cup cooked grains or starchy vegetables | 1 to 2 portions, depending on hunger and activity |
| **Healthy fats** | 1 thumb | About 1 tbsp oil, nut butter, nuts or seeds | 1 to 2 portions |

These are starting points, not strict limits. If you are very active, hungry or trying to build muscle, you may need more. Pay attention to your hunger and fullness signals: aim to finish meals feeling comfortably satisfied, not stuffed.
