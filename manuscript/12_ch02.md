@chapter 2 | Perimenopause: The Shifting Years
> Perimenopause can begin up to ten years before your final period, often while you're still being told you're too young for it.

%% Priya was forty-four and had a spreadsheet for everything: her team's deadlines, her son's soccer schedule, the family's grocery budget. What she couldn't organize was her body. Her period now arrived every twenty-two days, heavier each time. She woke at 3 a.m. with her T-shirt stuck to her back, then lay awake running through tomorrow's meetings. By ten each morning she'd hit a wall so hard she kept a stash of cookies in her desk drawer. When she mentioned the night sweats to a colleague, the answer came back with a laugh: "You're way too young for that." Priya laughed too. Then she went home and added a new tab to her spreadsheet: *symptoms*.

If you've been told you're too young for menopause while lying awake drenched in sweat, this chapter is for you.

Perimenopause is the part of the transition women know least about, and it's often the stage with the most noticeable symptoms. It usually begins in your forties, though for some women it starts in their late thirties. Because periods are still happening, many women (and, frustratingly, some healthcare providers) don't connect new symptoms with hormones at all. If that's what you've heard, this chapter is here to give you the fuller picture.

## The First Signs Are Easy to Miss

There's no single test that tells you perimenopause has started. It's usually recognized by a pattern, and the earliest changes are easy to blame on something else.

Your cycle is often the first thing to shift. Periods might start coming closer together, every 21 to 24 days instead of every 28, or further apart, with the odd skipped month. Bleeding can become heavier, lighter, longer or shorter than you're used to, and premenstrual symptoms may get worse. Doctors look for a difference of seven days or more between the length of one cycle and the next that happens again within the following ten cycles. It's one of the classic early markers. Later in the transition, gaps of 60 days or more become common.

Sleep often changes before anything else. You might find it harder to drift off, wake more often during the night, or wake very early and be unable to get back to sleep. Night sweats can play a part, but sleep can also become lighter without them, partly because progesterone, that natural calmer, is falling.

New or worsening anxiety, irritability and low mood are also very common, and they're often among the very first signs. Chapter 5 is devoted to them.

Heavy bleeding deserves a special mention. When estrogen swings high without regular progesterone to balance it, the lining of the uterus can build up more than usual, which can mean heavier periods or even flooding. Other women find their periods become lighter and shorter. Both can be normal, but very heavy bleeding should always be checked (see the end of this chapter).

## Hot, Achy, Bloated: What Else Can Change

As the transition goes on, other symptoms often join in. You might have some, many or very few of them.

- **Hot flashes and night sweats:** a sudden wave of heat through the face, neck and chest, sometimes with flushing, sweating, a racing heart and then chills. At night they're called night sweats, and doctors call both *vasomotor symptoms*.
- **Breast tenderness,** from estrogen peaks that are higher than usual.
- **Headaches and migraines,** especially if you've had hormone-related migraines before. They often improve after menopause.
- **Bloating and digestive changes,** because hormones affect fluid balance and the gut.
- **Joint aches,** particularly stiffness first thing in the morning.
- **Weight and shape changes,** often around the middle, even without changes in what you eat. Chapter 11 explains why.

### Why your internal thermostat goes haywire

Your brain has a thermostat in an area called the hypothalamus, and estrogen helps keep it stable. As estrogen swings and falls, the comfortable temperature zone narrows. Even a tiny rise in core temperature can then set off the full cooling response: blood vessels near the skin widen, you flush and sweat, and your heart speeds up. Researchers have pinpointed a group of brain cells, called KNDy neurons, that become overactive when estrogen is low. That discovery has led to a new class of non-hormonal treatments, which you'll meet in Chapter 6.

## The Symptoms Nobody Warned You About

Estrogen has receptors throughout the body, so perimenopause can show up in ways most women have never heard of. Some feel a fluttering, pounding or racing heartbeat, sometimes during a hot flash. Usually it's harmless, but palpitations should always be checked by a doctor, especially if you also feel dizzy or breathless or have chest pain. Others notice itchy, dry skin, or even a crawling sensation like insects on the skin, which has its own name: *formication*. Ringing in the ears (tinnitus) is reported by some women during the transition. Frozen shoulder, a painful stiffness that limits movement, is most common in women between 40 and 60, and hormonal change is a likely factor. Dry eyes, a dry mouth, tingling in the hands or feet, a burning feeling in the mouth and new allergies can all appear too.

None of these means something is seriously wrong. Each one deserves to be taken seriously and checked.

## The 10 a.m. Crash and the Blood-Sugar Connection

Many women describe a new kind of tiredness in perimenopause: not just sleepy, but drained, foggy and flat. Broken sleep is the obvious culprit. Heavy periods can lower your iron. Stress keeps your body on high alert. And changing estrogen levels can affect how sensitive your body is to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your cells.

When your body becomes a little less sensitive to insulin, a breakfast of toast and jam, a pastry or a sweetened coffee can send your blood sugar up quickly and then down just as fast. That dip shows up as an energy crash, irritability ("hangry") and a powerful craving for something sweet, which sets the whole cycle going again. Priya's 10 a.m. cookie stash wasn't a lack of discipline. It was her body asking for fuel.

This is one of the places where food can make the biggest difference. Meals that combine protein, fiber and healthy fats are digested more slowly and keep your energy steady. It's the heart of the Anchor, Add, Adapt method in Chapter 9.

## What to Put on Your Plate Now

Perimenopause is the time to start building habits that will serve you for decades, and a few nutrients deserve extra attention.

| Nutrient | Why it matters in perimenopause | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron** | Heavy periods can drain iron, leading to fatigue, breathlessness and hair shedding | Lean red meat, poultry, fish, lentils, beans, tofu, spinach, fortified cereals |
| **B vitamins** | Support energy production and the nervous system | Whole grains, eggs, dairy, legumes, leafy greens, meat and fish |
| **Magnesium** | Involved in muscle relaxation, sleep and blood-sugar control | Pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, spinach, black beans, whole grains, dark chocolate |
| **Protein** | Keeps you full, steadies blood sugar and protects muscle | Eggs, Greek yogurt, fish, poultry, tofu, tempeh, beans, lentils |
| **Fiber** | Supports digestion, fullness and steady energy | Vegetables, fruit, oats, beans, lentils, whole grains, seeds |

If your periods are heavy, ask your doctor to check your iron levels, including ferritin, which measures your iron stores. Low iron is very common in perimenopause and easy to miss. Pairing iron-rich plant foods with vitamin C, from peppers, tomatoes, citrus or berries, helps your body absorb more of it. Tea and coffee reduce absorption, so drink them between meals rather than with them. Please don't start iron supplements without checking first: too much iron can be harmful.

## Is It Perimenopause, or Something Else?

Many perimenopause symptoms overlap with other common conditions. That's why it matters not to assume everything is "just hormones," and equally not to accept that everything is "just stress."

These are the most common look-alikes, and each one is easy to check:

- **Thyroid problems.** An underactive thyroid is common in midlife women and can cause tiredness, weight gain, low mood, dry skin, thinning hair and changes to periods. An overactive one can cause anxiety, palpitations, sweating and poor sleep. A simple blood test checks both.
- **Low iron or anemia.** Fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness and feeling cold.
- **Low vitamin D or B12.** Tiredness, low mood and aches.
- **Diabetes.** Tiredness, thirst and needing to urinate often.
- **Sleep apnea.** More common after menopause; causes exhaustion, morning headaches and poor concentration.
- **Depression and anxiety disorders.** These can appear at any age, and the risk of depression rises in perimenopause, especially if you've had depression, postpartum depression or severe premenstrual symptoms before.

It's entirely possible to be in perimenopause *and* have one of these at the same time. A good doctor will consider both.

## When to Pick Up the Phone

You don't need to wait until your symptoms are unbearable. It's always reasonable to see a healthcare professional if your symptoms are affecting your life, and in particular if you notice:

[ ] Very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon every hour or two, passing large clots, or bleeding for more than seven days)
[ ] Bleeding between periods or after sex
[ ] Periods more often than every 21 days
[ ] Symptoms of perimenopause before the age of 45
[ ] Palpitations, especially with dizziness, chest pain or breathlessness
[ ] Low mood, anxiety or thoughts of self-harm (seek help urgently)
[ ] Symptoms disrupting your sleep, work or relationships

For women over 45 with typical symptoms, perimenopause is usually diagnosed from your symptoms and cycle pattern alone. Blood tests generally aren't needed and can mislead, because hormone levels change so much from day to day. They can be useful in specific situations, for example if you're under 45 or your doctor wants to rule out something else.
