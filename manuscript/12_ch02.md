@chapter 2 | Perimenopause: The Shifting Years
> The years before your final period can be the most confusing of all. Knowing what to look for changes everything.

Perimenopause is the part of the transition that most women know least about, and yet it is often the stage with the most noticeable symptoms. It usually begins in your forties, although for some women it starts in their late thirties. Because periods are still happening, many women (and, frustratingly, some healthcare providers) don't connect new symptoms with hormonal change at all.

If you have been told "you're too young for menopause" while lying awake drenched in sweat, this chapter is for you.

## Early Signs You Might Be in Perimenopause

There is no single test that tells you perimenopause has started. Instead, it is usually recognized by a pattern of changes. Some of the most common early signs include:

### Cycle changes

- Periods that come closer together (for example, every 21 to 24 days instead of every 28)
- Periods that are further apart, or skipped months
- Bleeding that is heavier, lighter, longer or shorter than you are used to
- Worse premenstrual symptoms than before

A change in the length of your cycle by seven days or more, persisting over several months, is one of the classic early markers of perimenopause. Later in the transition, gaps of 60 days or more between periods become common.

### Sleep disruption

Many women notice their sleep changing before anything else. You may find it harder to fall asleep, wake more often during the night, or wake very early and be unable to drift off again. Night sweats can play a part, but sleep can also become lighter even without them, partly because of falling progesterone.

### Anxiety and mood changes

New or worsening anxiety, irritability, low mood and a feeling of being overwhelmed are very common in perimenopause and are often among the first signs. Chapter 5 explores this in depth.

### Heavier or lighter periods

Fluctuating estrogen without the balancing effect of regular progesterone can cause the uterine lining to build up more than usual, leading to heavier bleeding or flooding. Other women find their periods become lighter and shorter. Both can be normal parts of perimenopause, but very heavy bleeding deserves attention (see "When to Talk to Your Doctor" below).

## Common Physical Changes

As the transition progresses, other symptoms often appear. You might experience some, many or very few of them.

- **Hot flashes and night sweats.** A sudden wave of heat, usually in the face, neck and chest, sometimes with flushing, sweating, a racing heart and then chills. When they happen at night, they are called night sweats. These are known medically as *vasomotor symptoms*.
- **Breast tenderness.** Higher-than-usual estrogen peaks can make breasts sore or swollen.
- **Headaches and migraines.** Women who had hormone-related migraines in the past may find they become more frequent during perimenopause, before often improving after menopause.
- **Bloating and digestive changes.** Hormonal fluctuations affect fluid balance and the gut, so bloating, constipation or a more sensitive stomach are common.
- **Joint aches.** Stiff, achy joints, especially in the morning, are surprisingly common.
- **Weight and shape changes.** Many women notice weight gathering around the middle, even without changes to how they eat. Chapter 11 explains why.

### Why hot flashes happen

Your brain has an internal thermostat in an area called the hypothalamus. Estrogen helps keep this thermostat stable. As estrogen fluctuates and falls, the comfortable temperature zone narrows, so even a tiny rise in core body temperature can trigger the body's cooling response: blood vessels near the skin widen, you flush and sweat, and your heart rate rises. Researchers have identified a group of brain cells (called KNDy neurons) that become overactive when estrogen is low, which has led to a new class of non-hormonal treatments described in Chapter 6.

## The Surprising Symptoms No One Warned You About

Because estrogen has receptors throughout the body, perimenopause can show up in ways most women have never heard of. These symptoms are real, they are common, and they are worth mentioning to your doctor:

- **Heart palpitations.** A fluttering, pounding or racing heartbeat, sometimes during a hot flash. Most of the time they are harmless, but palpitations should always be checked by a doctor to rule out other causes, especially if you also feel dizzy, breathless or have chest pain.
- **Itchy, dry or crawly skin.** Estrogen helps skin hold onto moisture and collagen. Some women experience itching or a sensation like insects crawling on the skin (called *formication*).
- **Tinnitus.** Ringing or buzzing in the ears is reported by some women during the transition.
- **Frozen shoulder.** Pain and stiffness in the shoulder joint that limits movement is most common in women aged 40 to 60, and hormonal change may be one reason why.
- **Dry eyes and dry mouth.**
- **Tingling in the hands or feet, or a burning feeling in the mouth.**
- **New allergies or sensitivities.**

None of these mean that something is seriously wrong, but each deserves to be taken seriously and assessed by a healthcare professional.

## Energy, Cravings and the Blood-Sugar Connection

Many women describe a new kind of tiredness in perimenopause: not just feeling sleepy, but feeling drained, foggy and flat. There are several reasons. Disrupted sleep is an obvious one. Heavy periods can lower iron levels. Stress keeps the body on high alert. And changing estrogen levels can affect how sensitive your body is to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your cells.

When your body becomes a little less sensitive to insulin, meals that are high in quickly digested carbohydrates (such as white bread, pastries, sweetened drinks or sugary cereals) can lead to a bigger rise in blood sugar followed by a sharper dip. That dip often shows up as an energy crash, irritability ("hangry" feelings) and strong cravings for something sweet, which can set the cycle going again.

The good news is that this is one of the areas where food can make the biggest difference. Meals that combine protein, fiber and healthy fats are digested more slowly and help keep energy steady. You will find this principle at the heart of the Menopause Plate in Chapter 9 and throughout the recipes.

## What Your Body Needs Most in This Stage

Perimenopause is the time to start building habits that will serve you for decades. A few nutrients deserve special attention now:

| Nutrient | Why it matters in perimenopause | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron** | Heavy periods can deplete iron, leading to fatigue, breathlessness and hair shedding | Lean red meat, poultry, fish, lentils, beans, tofu, spinach, fortified cereals |
| **B vitamins** | Support energy production and the nervous system | Whole grains, eggs, dairy, legumes, leafy greens, meat and fish |
| **Magnesium** | Involved in muscle relaxation, sleep and blood-sugar control | Pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, spinach, black beans, whole grains, dark chocolate |
| **Protein** | Keeps you full, steadies blood sugar and protects muscle | Eggs, Greek yogurt, fish, poultry, tofu, tempeh, beans, lentils |
| **Fiber** | Supports digestion, fullness and steady energy | Vegetables, fruit, oats, beans, lentils, whole grains, seeds |

### A note on iron

If your periods are heavy, it is worth asking your doctor to check your iron levels, including ferritin (your iron stores). Low iron is very common in perimenopause and easy to miss. Pairing iron-rich plant foods with a source of vitamin C, such as peppers, tomatoes, citrus or berries, helps your body absorb more of the iron. Tea and coffee can reduce iron absorption, so try to drink them between meals rather than with them. Please don't take iron supplements without checking with a doctor first, since too much iron can be harmful.

## Is It Perimenopause, or Something Else?

Many perimenopause symptoms overlap with other common health conditions. This is one reason it is so important not to assume that everything is "just hormones," and equally important not to be told that everything is "just stress."

### Thyroid changes, low iron and depression: overlapping signs worth checking

- **Thyroid problems.** An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) is common in midlife women and can cause tiredness, weight gain, low mood, dry skin, hair thinning and changes to periods. An overactive thyroid can cause anxiety, palpitations, sweating and sleep problems. A simple blood test can check your thyroid function.
- **Low iron or anemia.** Causes fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, feeling cold and hair loss.
- **Depression and anxiety disorders.** These can occur at any age, and the risk of depression rises during perimenopause, particularly in women with a history of depression, postnatal depression or severe premenstrual symptoms.
- **Low vitamin D or vitamin B12.** Both can cause tiredness, low mood and aches.
- **Diabetes.** Can cause tiredness, thirst and frequent urination.
- **Sleep apnea.** Becomes more common after menopause and can cause exhaustion, morning headaches and poor concentration.

It is entirely possible to be in perimenopause *and* have one of these conditions at the same time. A good doctor will consider both.

## When to Talk to Your Doctor

You do not need to wait until your symptoms are unbearable. It is always reasonable to talk to a healthcare professional if your symptoms are affecting your quality of life. In particular, make an appointment if you notice:

[ ] Very heavy bleeding (soaking through a pad or tampon every hour or two, passing large clots, or bleeding that lasts more than seven days)
[ ] Bleeding between periods or after sex
[ ] Periods that come more often than every 21 days
[ ] Symptoms of perimenopause before the age of 45
[ ] Palpitations, especially with dizziness, chest pain or breathlessness
[ ] Low mood, anxiety or thoughts of self-harm (seek help urgently)
[ ] Symptoms that are disrupting your sleep, work or relationships

For women over 45 with typical symptoms, perimenopause is usually diagnosed from your symptoms and cycle pattern alone. Blood tests are generally not needed and can be misleading because hormone levels change so much from day to day. They may be useful in specific situations, for example if you are under 45 or your doctor wants to rule out other conditions.

- Start a simple symptom diary. Each evening, note your cycle day, sleep quality, energy, mood and any hot flashes. After four to six weeks, patterns often start to appear, and the diary will make your doctor's appointment far more productive. (Appendix A has a tracker you can use.)
- Add a palm-sized portion of protein to your breakfast at least four days this week.

:::summary
- Perimenopause usually begins in your forties and lasts around four years on average, though it can be shorter or much longer.
- Early signs include changes to your cycle, disrupted sleep and new anxiety or low mood.
- Hot flashes are caused by a narrowing of the brain's comfortable temperature zone as estrogen fluctuates.
- Less familiar symptoms, such as palpitations, itchy skin, tinnitus and frozen shoulder, are real and worth discussing with a doctor.
- Steady meals with protein, fiber and healthy fats help protect your energy and reduce cravings.
- Iron, B vitamins, magnesium, protein and fiber deserve extra attention now.
- Some conditions, such as thyroid problems, low iron and depression, mimic perimenopause. It is worth checking.
