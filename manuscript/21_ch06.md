It isn't a substitute for personal medical advice: the right choice depends on your symptoms, your health history, your preferences and your values.

## Hormone Therapy: The Treatment With the Strongest Track Record

Menopausal hormone therapy, often called hormone replacement therapy (HRT), replaces some of the hormones your ovaries no longer make in the same amounts. It's the **most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats** (established), and it also helps prevent bone loss and fractures.

### What really happened in 2002

That year, early results from the estrogen-plus-progestin arm of a large American study called the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) were widely reported as showing that hormone therapy raised the risk of breast cancer, heart disease and stroke. Prescriptions collapsed around the world almost overnight.

Since then, much more careful analysis has revealed a more nuanced picture. The women in the WHI were 63 on average, many years past menopause, and many already had risk factors. For healthy women who start hormone therapy before 60, or within ten years of menopause, the benefits generally outweigh the risks. That's now the position of The Menopause Society and other major organizations (established).

In November 2025, the US Food and Drug Administration updated its warnings to reflect more recent evidence, a change some experts had called for and others debated. It asked manufacturers to remove heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from the boxed warning on menopausal hormone therapy, and to drop the advice to use "the lowest dose for the shortest time." The first updated labels were approved in February 2026, with others following. The risks haven't disappeared: heart disease, stroke, blood clots and breast cancer are still described in the prescribing information, just no longer in the boxed warning. And the boxed warning about endometrial cancer stays for estrogen-only therapy in women who still have a uterus, which is why they need a progestogen too.

### The different types, and who they may suit

**Estrogen** is the ingredient that relieves symptoms. It can be taken as a tablet, or through the skin as a patch, gel or spray, which doctors call *transdermal* estrogen. If you still have a uterus, you also need **a progestogen** (progesterone or a similar hormone) to protect the uterine lining from overgrowth, which could otherwise raise the risk of endometrial cancer; that can be a tablet, part of a combined patch, or a hormonal IUD. Women who've had a hysterectomy can usually take **estrogen alone**. And **testosterone** is sometimes prescribed for women whose low sexual desire is causing distress, once other factors have been addressed.

Many women notice relief from hot flashes and night sweats within a few weeks, and often better sleep, mood and quality of life. Hormone therapy also prevents bone loss and lowers fracture risk, and for some women it eases vaginal dryness and joint aches.

### The risks, in proportion

- **Blood clots and stroke.** Estrogen tablets slightly increase the risk of blood clots. Transdermal estrogen at standard doses doesn't appear to raise this risk in the same way (established), so it's often preferred for women with risk factors.
- **Breast cancer.** Combined therapy (estrogen with a progestogen) is linked to a small increase in breast cancer risk that rises with longer use, while estrogen-only therapy appears to carry little or no increase. In absolute terms, that's fewer than one extra case per 1,000 women for each year of combined therapy, and the risk falls again after stopping. For context, the extra risk from combined therapy is similar to or smaller than the extra risk linked to regularly drinking two or more alcoholic drinks a day, or to obesity.
- **Side effects** such as breast tenderness, bloating, headaches or irregular bleeding are common in the first few months. They often settle, or can be eased by changing the dose or type.

Hormone therapy usually isn't recommended for women who've had breast cancer, certain types of blood clots, stroke, heart disease or active liver disease, although specialists may advise differently in individual cases.

:::evidence Hormone therapy at a glance
**Established:** Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats, and it prevents bone loss. For most healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, benefits outweigh risks. Women with premature or early menopause are generally advised to take it until at least the average age of menopause, unless there's a reason not to.
**Emerging:** Whether starting hormone therapy early has lasting benefits for the heart or brain is still being studied. It isn't currently recommended solely to prevent heart disease or dementia.
:::

### "Bioidentical" hormones: read the label

You may see compounded "bioidentical" hormones marketed as more natural or safer. Some regulated, approved products are chemically identical to your body's own hormones (such as estradiol and micronized progesterone), and these are widely prescribed. Custom-compounded products made in individual pharmacies, though, aren't tested and regulated in the same way, so their dose, purity and safety can vary. Major menopause organizations recommend regulated, approved products wherever possible.

## Comfort Where It Counts: Vaginal Estrogen

For vaginal dryness, painful sex and bladder symptoms (genitourinary syndrome of menopause, from Chapter 3), **low-dose vaginal estrogen** works very well (established). It comes as a cream, a small tablet or vaginal insert, or a soft ring that stays in place for three months. The FDA is also removing the boxed warning from low-dose vaginal estrogen labels; the ring was the first to be updated, in February 2026.

Because the dose is very low and acts locally, only tiny amounts reach the bloodstream. That's why it's considered safe for most women, can be used long-term, and doesn't usually need a progestogen alongside it. You can use it together with systemic hormone therapy, and many women who can't or don't want to take systemic hormones can still use it. If you've had breast cancer, discuss it with your oncology team.

Other options include non-hormonal moisturizers and lubricants, vaginal DHEA (prasterone), a nightly vaginal insert; ospemifene, a daily pill taken by mouth that carries its own boxed warning; and pelvic floor physical therapy.

## When Hormones Aren't Right for You

Not every woman can or wants to take hormone therapy, perhaps because of breast cancer or another hormone-sensitive cancer, a history of blood clots or stroke, or simple preference. Happily, there are now several effective non-hormonal medicines for hot flashes and night sweats (established unless noted).

- **Neurokinin receptor antagonists.** A newer class of medicines, including fezolinetant and elinzanetant, works directly on the overactive temperature-control cells described in Chapter 2. In trials they significantly reduced the number and severity of hot flashes. Both require liver blood tests. Fezolinetant has carried a boxed warning for rare but serious liver injury since December 2024, with tests before you start, monthly for the first three months, then at six and nine months. Elinzanetant, approved by the FDA in October 2025, needs tests before you start and at three months, and it can cause sleepiness. Longer-term safety data for both are still being gathered.
- **Certain antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs).** Low doses of medicines such as paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram, venlafaxine and desvenlafaxine can reduce hot flashes even in women who aren't depressed, and some help with mood and anxiety too. Some SSRIs interact with tamoxifen, so women taking it need specialist advice.
- **Gabapentin.** An anti-seizure and nerve-pain medicine that can reduce hot flashes, particularly at night. It often causes drowsiness, which can actually help if night sweats wreck your sleep.
- **Oxybutynin.** A bladder medicine that can also reduce hot flashes, although it may not suit older women because of possible effects on memory.
- **Clonidine.** A blood pressure medicine once used for hot flashes. The Menopause Society (2023) no longer recommends it, because its effect is small and side effects are common.

## Weight-Loss Medications: What to Know If You're Using or Considering Them

A newer group of medicines, known as GLP-1 receptor agonists (and related drugs that act on similar hormones), has become widely prescribed for type 2 diabetes and, for some people, for weight management. They're included here for information only, not as a recommendation. Whether they're right for you is a decision for you and your doctor.

### What they are and what to ask

These medicines mimic natural hormones that help regulate appetite and blood sugar, so people tend to feel full sooner and for longer. They're usually prescribed for adults with type 2 diabetes, or for weight management in adults whose weight is affecting their health, according to criteria set by regulators and guidelines. If you're thinking about one, useful questions to ask include: Is it suitable for me, given my health history and other medicines? What are the possible side effects, and what should I do if I get them? How will we monitor my progress, including my muscle and bone health? What happens if I stop?

### The side effects to know

The most common side effects are digestive: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and constipation, especially when starting or increasing the dose. Less common but more serious effects, including gallbladder problems and inflammation of the pancreas, have also been reported, and your doctor will explain what to watch for.

- **Boxed warning on thyroid tumors.** In the United States, these medicines carry a boxed warning, the FDA's most prominent safety warning, because they caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rodent studies. It isn't known whether this happens in humans, but they shouldn't be used by anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN 2). Tell your doctor about any lump or swelling in your neck, trouble swallowing or persistent hoarseness.
- **Pregnancy and contraception.** You can still become pregnant during perimenopause, and these medicines aren't recommended in pregnancy. If you're planning a pregnancy, your doctor will advise when to stop beforehand. Tirzepatide can make birth-control pills less effective. Its prescribing information advises switching to a non-oral method, or adding a barrier method, for four weeks after starting it and for four weeks after each dose increase. Ask your doctor what applies to the medicine you're taking.
- **Weight lost isn't all fat.** In clinical trials, a substantial share of the weight lost was "lean mass." That includes muscle, but also water, organs and other non-fat tissue, so it isn't all muscle. Even so, midlife women are already losing muscle more quickly, which makes protecting it a priority.
- **What's still unknown.** There's limited research on these medicines specifically in women going through the menopause transition, including their long-term effects on bone.

### If you go ahead

If you and your doctor decide on this path, the principles in this book become even more important. Include protein at every meal, around 20 to 30 grams, and eat it first while your appetite is smallest. Keep up strength training at least twice a week to protect muscle and bone. With smaller portions, make every bite count with vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, dairy or fortified alternatives, fish and lean protein. Drink plenty of fluid, increase fiber gradually to help with constipation, and ask whether a registered dietitian can support you.

## Changing How Hot Flashes Feel: CBT and Hypnosis

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a structured, practical talking therapy that helps you notice and change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior. Menopause-specific CBT doesn't usually reduce how *often* hot flashes happen, but it significantly reduces how much they *bother* you and interfere with daily life (established). It also helps with sleep, low mood and anxiety. The Menopause Society recommends it as a non-hormonal option, and in the UK, NICE guidance says it can be considered for menopause symptoms. CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment for long-term sleep problems in adults, and it works well during the transition. You can access CBT through a therapist, a group program, a book or an evidence-based online course.

**Clinical hypnosis,** delivered by a trained practitioner or through guided recordings, has also been shown to reduce hot flashes in trials, and The Menopause Society recommends it as a non-hormonal option (established).

## The Supplement Aisle, Decoded

The supplement aisle is full of products promising menopause relief. Some are harmless but ineffective, some have limited evidence, and a few can interact with medicines or cause side effects. "Natural" doesn't always mean safe, and supplements aren't tested and regulated as strictly as medicines.

| Supplement | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| **Soy isoflavones** | Emerging: may modestly reduce hot flash frequency for some women; soy foods are a good first step |
| **Black cohosh** | Mixed and inconsistent results; rare reports of liver problems; not recommended by The Menopause Society |
| **Red clover, dong quai, evening primrose oil, wild yam** | Little or no good evidence of benefit; wild yam creams don't convert to progesterone in the body |
| **St. John's wort** | Some evidence for mood, but serious interactions with many medicines, including tamoxifen, blood thinners and oral contraceptives |
| **Vitamin D** | Established: important for bone health; many people need a supplement, especially in winter |
| **Calcium** | Best from food; supplements may be advised if your diet falls short |
| **Omega-3 (fish oil)** | Good for heart health as part of the diet; doesn't appear to reduce hot flashes |

### Creatine, collagen and magnesium: hype or help?

These three are hugely popular with women in midlife. **Creatine** (emerging) is well researched in athletes. It helps muscles produce energy for short bursts of effort, and in older adults, combining it with strength training may lead to bigger strength gains than training alone. Research in menopausal women specifically, including possible effects on bone, mood and the brain, is still limited and mixed. It appears safe for most healthy people at typical doses of around 3 to 5 grams a day, but check with your doctor if you have kidney problems.

**Collagen** (emerging, limited) may modestly improve skin hydration and elasticity in some studies, but many are funded by manufacturers, and better independent research is needed. It's a protein, but it lacks some essential amino acids, so it can't replace protein from food when it comes to building muscle.

**Magnesium** (emerging, limited) is essential for muscle and nerve function, and many people don't get enough from food. Evidence that supplements improve sleep or menopausal symptoms is limited, so a food-first approach, with nuts, seeds, beans, whole grains and leafy greens, is a safe place to start. Supplements can cause digestive upset and interact with some medicines.

Before taking any supplement, check with your doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take prescription medicines, have a medical condition or have had cancer.

## Feeling Unheard? How to Get the Appointment You Need

Many women say they've been dismissed, misdiagnosed or told "it's just your age." Menopause training for healthcare professionals has historically been thin, though it's improving. You deserve to be heard, and preparation changes the conversation. It's why Mei walked into her appointment with a list. A few steps make a big difference:

1. **Prepare.** Bring your symptom diary or a list of symptoms, how often they happen and how they affect your life. Be specific: "I wake three or four times a night drenched in sweat, and I've had to cancel work twice this month" is far more powerful than "I'm not sleeping well."
2. **Say the word.** Ask directly: "Could this be perimenopause or menopause?"
3. **Prioritize.** If time is short, start with the one or two symptoms that bother you most.
4. **Ask about every option.** "What are all my treatment options, hormonal and non-hormonal? What are the benefits and risks for someone like me?"
5. **Bring support.** A partner or friend can help you remember what was said.
6. **Take notes,** or ask whether you can record the conversation.
7. **Ask for a follow-up** to review how a treatment is working, typically after three months.
8. **Get a second opinion** if you feel unheard. You can ask to see a clinician with a special interest in menopause; The Menopause Society has a directory of certified practitioners.

## Questions to Take to Your Doctor

Photograph or copy this list for your next appointment, as Mei did.

[ ] Are my symptoms likely to be caused by perimenopause or menopause?
[ ] Do I need any tests, and why?
[ ] Is hormone therapy a suitable option for me? What are the benefits and risks in my situation?
[ ] Which type of hormone therapy would you recommend, and why?
[ ] What non-hormonal options could help my symptoms?
[ ] Could vaginal estrogen help with dryness, discomfort or bladder symptoms?
[ ] Could any of my current medicines or supplements affect my symptoms or interact with treatment?
[ ] Do I need a bone density scan, or cholesterol, blood sugar or thyroid tests?
[ ] Can you refer me for CBT, pelvic floor therapy or a menopause specialist?
[ ] When should we review how things are going?
