@chapter 6 | Treatments and Therapies: Knowing Your Options
> Information is power. The more you know about your options, the better the conversations you can have with your doctor.

Nutrition and lifestyle can make a real difference to how you feel during the menopause transition, and the rest of this book will show you how. But for many women, food and exercise alone are not enough, especially for moderate to severe hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes or vaginal symptoms. There is no prize for suffering in silence, and choosing medical treatment is not a failure.

This chapter offers a balanced, evidence-informed overview of the main treatments available, so you can talk them through confidently with your healthcare provider. It is not a substitute for personal medical advice: the right choice for you depends on your symptoms, health history, preferences and values.

:::note How to read this chapter
Throughout, we label evidence as **established** (supported by strong, consistent research and major guidelines) or **emerging** (promising, but not yet settled). Treatments and guidelines change as research develops, and availability and brand names differ between countries.
:::

## Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT/HRT)

Menopausal hormone therapy, often called hormone replacement therapy (HRT), replaces some of the hormones your ovaries no longer produce in the same amounts. It is the **most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats** (established), and it also helps prevent bone loss and fractures.

### A brief history: why so many women are wary

In 2002, early results from a large American study called the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) were widely reported as showing that hormone therapy increased the risk of breast cancer, heart disease and stroke. Prescriptions dropped dramatically around the world almost overnight.

Since then, much more careful analysis has shown a more nuanced picture. The women in the WHI were, on average, 63 years old, many years past menopause, and many had existing risk factors. For healthy women who start hormone therapy before the age of 60, or within ten years of menopause, the benefits generally outweigh the risks. This is the current position of The Menopause Society and other major organizations (established).

### Types, benefits, risks and who it may suit

Hormone therapy comes in several forms:

- **Estrogen** is the main ingredient that relieves symptoms. It can be taken as a tablet, or through the skin as a patch, gel or spray (called *transdermal* estrogen).
- **A progestogen** (progesterone or a similar hormone) is needed if you still have a uterus, to protect the lining of the uterus from overgrowth, which could otherwise increase the risk of endometrial cancer. This can be a tablet, part of a combined patch, or a hormonal IUD.
- Women who have had a hysterectomy can usually take **estrogen alone**.
- **Testosterone** is sometimes prescribed for women with low sexual desire that is causing distress, when other factors have been addressed.

**Possible benefits:**

- relief of hot flashes and night sweats, often within a few weeks
- better sleep, mood and quality of life for many women
- prevention of bone loss and reduced risk of fractures
- improvement in vaginal dryness and joint aches for some women

**Possible risks and side effects:**

- **Blood clots and stroke.** Estrogen taken as a tablet slightly increases the risk of blood clots. Transdermal estrogen (patches, gels or sprays) at standard doses does not appear to increase this risk in the same way (established), so it is often preferred for women with risk factors.
- **Breast cancer.** Combined hormone therapy (estrogen with a progestogen) is associated with a small increase in breast cancer risk that rises with longer use. Estrogen-only therapy appears to have little or no increase in risk. To put this in context, the increase in risk from combined therapy is similar to or smaller than the increase linked to regularly drinking two or more alcoholic drinks a day, or to obesity.
- **Side effects** such as breast tenderness, bloating, headaches or irregular bleeding are common in the first few months and often settle, or can be helped by adjusting the dose or type.

Hormone therapy is not usually recommended for women who have had breast cancer, certain types of blood clots, stroke, heart disease or active liver disease, though specialist advice may differ in individual situations.

:::evidence Hormone therapy at a glance
**Established:** Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats, and it prevents bone loss. For most healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, benefits outweigh risks. Women with premature or early menopause are generally advised to take hormone therapy until at least the average age of menopause, unless there is a reason not to.
**Emerging:** Whether starting hormone therapy early has lasting benefits for the heart or brain is still being studied. Hormone therapy is not currently recommended solely to prevent heart disease or dementia.
:::

### "Bioidentical" hormones: a word of caution

You may see compounded "bioidentical" hormones advertised as more natural or safer. Some regulated, approved hormone products are chemically identical to the body's own hormones (such as estradiol and micronized progesterone), and these are widely prescribed. However, custom-compounded products made in individual pharmacies are not tested and regulated in the same way, so their dose, purity and safety can vary. Major menopause organizations recommend regulated, approved products wherever possible.

## Vaginal Estrogen and Genitourinary Health

For vaginal dryness, discomfort during sex and bladder symptoms (genitourinary syndrome of menopause, described in Chapter 3), **low-dose vaginal estrogen** is very effective (established). It comes as a cream, a small tablet or pessary inserted into the vagina, or a soft ring that stays in place for three months.

Because the dose is very low and acts locally, only tiny amounts reach the bloodstream. For this reason, it is considered safe for most women, can be used long-term, and does not usually require a progestogen. It can be used alongside systemic hormone therapy, and many women who cannot or prefer not to take systemic hormones can still use it. Women who have had breast cancer should discuss it with their oncology team.

Other options for genitourinary symptoms include non-hormonal vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, vaginal DHEA (prasterone), a tablet called ospemifene, and pelvic floor physical therapy.

## Non-Hormonal Prescription Options

Not every woman can or wants to take hormone therapy. Fortunately, there are now several effective non-hormonal medicines for hot flashes and night sweats.

### When hormone therapy isn't an option for you

Women who have had breast cancer or other hormone-sensitive cancers, a history of blood clots or stroke, or who simply prefer to avoid hormones, have several options (established unless noted):

- **Neurokinin receptor antagonists.** A newer class of medicines, including fezolinetant and elinzanetant, works directly on the overactive temperature-control cells in the brain described in Chapter 2. In clinical trials they significantly reduced the number and severity of hot flashes. Because they are new, longer-term safety data are still being gathered, and your doctor may monitor your liver function.
- **Certain antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs).** Low doses of medicines such as paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram, venlafaxine and desvenlafaxine can reduce hot flashes, even in women who are not depressed. Some are also helpful for mood and anxiety. Note that some SSRIs can interact with tamoxifen, so women taking it need specialist advice.
- **Gabapentin.** An anti-seizure and nerve-pain medicine that can reduce hot flashes, particularly at night. Drowsiness is a common side effect, which may actually help if night sweats are disrupting sleep.
- **Oxybutynin.** A bladder medicine that can also reduce hot flashes, although it may not be suitable for older women because of possible effects on memory.
- **Clonidine.** A blood pressure medicine with a modest effect on hot flashes; side effects often limit its use.

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) for Hot Flashes and Sleep

CBT is a structured, practical form of talking therapy that helps you notice and change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior. CBT designed specifically for menopause does not usually reduce how *often* hot flashes happen, but it significantly reduces how much they *bother* you and interfere with daily life (established). It also helps with sleep, low mood and anxiety. NICE guidelines now recommend that CBT be offered for menopause symptoms.

CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line recommended treatment for long-term sleep problems in adults, and it works well during the menopause transition. CBT can be accessed through a therapist, a structured group, a book or an evidence-based online program.

**Clinical hypnosis**, delivered by a trained practitioner or through guided recordings, has also been shown to reduce hot flashes in clinical trials and is recommended by The Menopause Society as a non-hormonal option (established).

## Supplements: What's Supported, What's Unproven, What to Be Careful With

The supplement aisle is full of products promising menopause relief. Some are harmless but ineffective, some have limited evidence, and a few can interact with medications or cause side effects. "Natural" does not always mean safe, and supplements are not tested and regulated as strictly as medicines.

| Supplement | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| **Soy isoflavones** | Emerging: may modestly reduce hot flash frequency for some women; soy foods are a good first step |
| **Black cohosh** | Mixed and inconsistent results; rare reports of liver problems; not recommended by The Menopause Society |
| **Red clover, dong quai, evening primrose oil, wild yam** | Little or no good evidence of benefit; wild yam creams do not convert to progesterone in the body |
| **St. John's wort** | Some evidence for mood, but serious interactions with many medicines, including tamoxifen, blood thinners and oral contraceptives |
| **Vitamin D** | Established: important for bone health; many people need a supplement, especially in winter |
| **Calcium** | Best from food; supplements may be advised if your diet falls short |
| **Omega-3 (fish oil)** | Good for heart health as part of the diet; does not appear to reduce hot flashes |

### Creatine, collagen and magnesium: hype or help?

These three supplements are hugely popular with women in midlife. Here is where the evidence currently stands:

- **Creatine (emerging).** A well-researched supplement among athletes, creatine helps muscles produce energy during short bursts of effort. In older adults, combining creatine with strength training may lead to greater gains in muscle strength than training alone. Research in menopausal women specifically, including possible effects on bone, mood and brain function, is still limited and mixed. It appears safe for most healthy people at typical doses (around 3 to 5 grams a day), but check with your doctor if you have kidney problems.
- **Collagen (emerging, limited).** Some studies suggest collagen supplements may modestly improve skin hydration and elasticity, but many are funded by manufacturers, and higher-quality independent research is needed. Collagen is a protein, but it lacks some essential amino acids, so it is not a substitute for protein from food when it comes to building muscle.
- **Magnesium (emerging, limited).** Magnesium is essential for muscle and nerve function, and many people don't get enough from their diet. Evidence that magnesium supplements improve sleep or menopausal symptoms is limited. A food-first approach, with nuts, seeds, beans, whole grains and leafy greens, is a safe place to start. Magnesium supplements can cause digestive upset and interact with some medicines.

Before taking any supplement, check with your doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take prescription medications, have a medical condition or have had cancer.

## Feeling Unheard? How to Advocate for Yourself at Appointments

Many women say they have been dismissed, misdiagnosed or told "it's just your age." Menopause training for healthcare professionals has historically been limited, though it is improving. You deserve to be listened to, and a few simple steps can make your appointment far more productive:

1. **Prepare.** Bring your symptom diary or a list of your symptoms, how often they happen and how they affect your life. Be specific: "I wake three to four times a night drenched in sweat, and I've had to cancel work twice this month" is far more powerful than "I'm not sleeping well."
2. **Say the word.** Ask directly: "Could this be perimenopause or menopause?"
3. **Prioritize.** If time is short, start with the one or two symptoms that bother you most.
4. **Ask about options.** "What are all my treatment options, including hormonal and non-hormonal ones? What are the benefits and risks for someone like me?"
5. **Bring support.** A partner or friend can help you remember what was said.
6. **Take notes,** or ask if you can record the conversation.
7. **Ask for a follow-up** to review how a treatment is working, typically after three months.
8. **Seek a second opinion** if you feel unheard. You can ask to see a clinician with a special interest in menopause. The Menopause Society has a directory of certified menopause practitioners.

## Questions to Ask Your Doctor

Photograph or copy this list to take to your next appointment.

[ ] Are my symptoms likely to be caused by perimenopause or menopause?
[ ] Do I need any tests, and why?
[ ] Is hormone therapy a suitable option for me? What are the benefits and risks in my situation?
[ ] Which type of hormone therapy would you recommend, and why?
[ ] What non-hormonal options could help my symptoms?
[ ] Could vaginal estrogen help with dryness, discomfort or bladder symptoms?
[ ] Are any of my current medicines or supplements likely to affect my symptoms or interact with treatment?
[ ] Do I need a bone density scan, or cholesterol, blood sugar or thyroid tests?
[ ] Can you refer me for CBT, pelvic floor therapy or a menopause specialist?
[ ] When should we review how things are going?
