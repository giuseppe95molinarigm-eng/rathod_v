@chapter 4 | Post-Menopause: Thriving Beyond
> One in two women over fifty will break a bone because of osteoporosis. Most never feel it coming.

%% Linda slipped on the icy step outside her daughter's house in February, put out a hand to catch herself, and heard the crack before she felt it. She was sixty-one, walked her dog every day, and considered herself "pretty healthy." At the hospital, the doctor looked at the X-ray of her wrist and asked a question nobody had asked her before. Had she ever had a bone density scan? She hadn't. Six weeks later, cast off and scan done, she learned the word *osteopenia*. "I thought bones were just… bones," she told her daughter. "I didn't know they could be fading without a single symptom." Then she asked the question that brought her to this chapter: "Is it too late to do something about it?"

It isn't. Not for Linda, and not for you.

Once you've passed the twelve-month milestone, you're post-menopausal for the rest of your life. For many women, this stage brings a welcome calm: no more periods, hormones settled, the unpredictable swings of perimenopause behind you. Plenty of women describe their fifties and sixties as a time of new freedom and clarity. At the same time, life with less estrogen brings long-term changes to your bones, heart, muscles and brain that are worth understanding. These are exactly the areas where food and movement make the biggest difference.

## What Fades and What Stays

Every woman is different, but some patterns are common. Mood swings tied to hormonal fluctuation tend to settle, along with breast tenderness and hormone-related migraines. For most women, hot flashes and night sweats eventually ease, although they can carry on for years. Other symptoms may continue or even appear later: vaginal dryness and discomfort, bladder changes and urinary infections, stiff joints, dry skin and lighter sleep. If anything is still affecting your quality of life, talk to your doctor. There's no age limit on feeling comfortable.

## Your Bones Are Alive

Bone is living tissue. All your life, your body breaks down old bone and builds new bone, and estrogen helps keep that process in balance. When estrogen falls, breakdown overtakes building, and bone density starts to slip.

Women can lose up to 20 percent of their bone density in the five to seven years after menopause. Over time, that can lead to **osteoporosis**, a condition in which bones become fragile and break more easily. Hip, spine and wrist fractures are the most common, and hip fractures in particular can seriously affect independence. Osteoporosis is often called a silent disease because there are no symptoms until a bone breaks. That's why prevention matters so much, and why, like Linda, it's never too late to start.

### What protects your bones

Bones respond to load. Walking, dancing and climbing stairs all send a "stay strong" signal, but **strength training** sends the loudest one. In trials of supervised, progressive strength training in post-menopausal women with low bone density, bone density and physical function improved. Food matters too: aim for around 1,200 mg of calcium a day, mostly from food, and enough vitamin D to absorb it (many people need a supplement, so ask your doctor). Around half of bone's volume is protein, so protein supports bone strength as well, especially alongside enough calcium.

Smoking and heavy drinking both speed up bone loss. And because the real danger is the fracture, preventing falls counts: balance training, good lighting at home and sensible footwear all reduce your risk. When needed, medical treatment works well. Hormone therapy helps prevent bone loss, and there are several effective medications for osteoporosis. A bone density scan helps your doctor decide what's right for you.

## Your Heart Needs You Now

Heart disease is the leading cause of death in women, and the risk rises after menopause. Before menopause, estrogen helps keep blood vessels flexible and cholesterol favorable. Afterward, LDL ("bad") cholesterol tends to climb, blood pressure often creeps up, and the body stores more fat around the abdomen, which is linked to heart disease and type 2 diabetes.

Women's heart attack symptoms can look different from the classic crushing chest pain. They may include breathlessness, nausea, unusual tiredness, discomfort in the jaw, neck, back or arm, or a feeling like indigestion. If you notice these, get urgent medical help.

The good news is that your heart responds to the same habits that run through this whole book:

- **Eat plenty of plants.** Vegetables, fruit, beans, lentils, whole grains, nuts and seeds, especially in a Mediterranean-style pattern, have some of the strongest evidence for heart health.
- **Get soluble fiber** from oats, barley, beans and lentils, which helps lower LDL cholesterol.
- **Swap your fats.** Choose olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish over the saturated fats in processed meats, fried foods and pastries.
- **Cook more at home** to cut the salt that pushes blood pressure up.
- **Move for about 150 minutes a week,** for example with brisk walking.
- **Don't smoke,** keep alcohol low, manage stress and protect your sleep.

## Muscle Is Your Retirement Plan

From around the age of 30 we gradually lose muscle mass and strength, a process that speeds up around menopause and is called **sarcopenia** when it becomes significant. Muscle matters far beyond appearance. It gets you up from a chair, carries your shopping, keeps your balance, lets you play with grandchildren and keeps you independent. It also helps control blood sugar and supports your bones.

Two things protect muscle better than anything else:

1. **Strength training** at least twice a week, using your body weight, bands, free weights or machines. Part Six includes two short no-equipment sessions a week.
2. **Enough protein,** spread through the day. Many experts now suggest that older adults aim for about 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight daily, possibly more if you're very active or recovering from illness. Spread it out, with around 20 to 30 grams at each meal.

Balance naturally declines with age too, but it improves quickly with practice. Standing on one leg while you brush your teeth, and the balance poses in the yoga plan, make a real difference.

## Keeping Your Brain Sharp

Many women worry about memory during the transition. The brain fog of perimenopause is real, but research suggests it's usually temporary, with memory and concentration often improving after menopause.

Looking further ahead, women are more likely than men to develop dementia, and researchers are actively studying the role of hormones. There's still a lot to learn, but the habits that protect your heart also protect your brain:

- regular physical activity
- a Mediterranean-style diet full of vegetables, berries, leafy greens, beans, nuts, olive oil and fish
- good sleep
- keeping blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol in check
- staying socially connected and mentally engaged
- protecting your hearing (hearing loss is a recognized risk factor for dementia)
- not smoking, and keeping alcohol low

## What to Put on Your Plate Now

| Nutrient | Why it matters now | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin B12** | Absorption declines with age and with some medicines (such as metformin and acid reducers); low levels cause fatigue and nerve problems | Fish, meat, eggs, dairy, fortified plant milks and cereals, nutritional yeast |
| **Calcium** | Supports bone strength | Dairy, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, leafy greens |
| **Vitamin D** | Helps absorb calcium; supports muscle | Oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; often a supplement |
| **Omega-3 fats** | Support heart and brain health | Salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout, walnuts, ground flaxseed, chia seeds |
| **Protein (a little more)** | Protects muscle and bone with age | Spread across every meal and snack |

Your iron needs fall after menopause, from around 18 mg to 8 mg a day, because you're no longer losing blood every month. Unless your doctor advises otherwise, most post-menopausal women don't need an iron supplement, which is why many multivitamins for women over 50 contain little or none.

## Know Your Numbers

Midlife is a great time to get a clear picture of your health, so you and your doctor can spot changes early, when they're easiest to deal with.

| Check | Why it matters | How often (general guide) |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood pressure** | Rarely causes symptoms but raises the risk of heart disease and stroke | At least once a year, or as advised |
| **Cholesterol (lipid panel)** | LDL cholesterol often rises after menopause | Every few years, or more often if raised |
| **Blood sugar (HbA1c or fasting glucose)** | Catches prediabetes and diabetes early | Every few years from midlife, or as advised |
| **Bone density (DXA) scan** | Measures bone density and fracture risk | Recommended from 65, or earlier if you have risk factors |
| **Waist measurement** | A simple marker of abdominal fat and metabolic risk | Whenever you like, as a check-in |
| **Vitamin D, B12, thyroid** | Common deficiencies and conditions in midlife | If you have symptoms or risk factors |
| **Cancer screening** | Breast, cervical and colorectal screening save lives | As your national screening program recommends |

Earlier testing is often advised if you have risk factors. These include early menopause, a low body weight, smoking, a parent who broke a hip, long-term steroid use, or a broken bone from a minor fall, as Linda had. Recommendations vary by country, so ask your doctor what's right for you.

- Heart disease risk rises after menopause. Plant-rich eating, fiber, healthy fats, movement and not smoking all help.
- Muscle is one of your greatest assets for healthy aging. Protect it with strength training and protein at every meal.
