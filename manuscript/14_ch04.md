@chapter 4 | Post-Menopause: Thriving Beyond
> Life after menopause is not an ending. It is a new chapter, and it can be one of the most confident, energetic and freeing of all.

Once you have passed the twelve-month milestone, you are post-menopausal for the rest of your life. For many women, this stage brings a welcome sense of calm. Periods are gone, hormone levels have settled, and the unpredictable ups and downs of perimenopause are behind you. Many women describe a renewed sense of freedom and clarity in their fifties and sixties.

At the same time, life with lower estrogen brings some long-term changes that are worth understanding, particularly for your bones, heart, muscles and brain. The good news is that these are also the areas where daily habits, especially nutrition and movement, have the most powerful effect.

## Which Symptoms Ease and Which May Linger

Every woman is different, but in general:

- **Symptoms that often ease over time:** mood swings linked to hormonal fluctuation, breast tenderness, hormone-related migraines, and, for most women, hot flashes and night sweats (although these can continue for many years).
- **Symptoms that may continue or appear later:** vaginal dryness and discomfort, bladder changes and urinary tract infections, joint stiffness, dry skin and changes in sleep.

If any symptom continues to affect your quality of life, it is worth discussing with your doctor. There is no age limit on feeling comfortable.

## Protecting Your Bones

Bone is living tissue. Throughout life, your body constantly breaks down old bone and builds new bone. Estrogen helps keep this process in balance. When estrogen falls, bone is broken down faster than it is rebuilt, and bone density begins to decline.

### Osteoporosis risk and what helps

Women can lose up to 20 percent of their bone density in the five to seven years following menopause. Over time, this can lead to **osteoporosis**, a condition in which bones become fragile and more likely to break. According to bone health organizations, around one in two women over 50 will break a bone because of osteoporosis during their lifetime. Fractures of the hip, spine and wrist are the most common, and hip fractures in particular can seriously affect independence.

Osteoporosis is often called a "silent" disease because there are no symptoms until a bone breaks. That is why prevention matters so much, and why it is never too late to start.

What helps:

- **Weight-bearing and strength exercise.** Bones respond to load. Walking, dancing, stair climbing and, especially, strength training send signals to your bones to stay strong. Studies of supervised, progressive strength training in postmenopausal women with low bone density have shown improvements in bone density and physical function.
- **Calcium.** Aim for around 1,200 mg a day, mainly from food.
- **Vitamin D.** Helps you absorb calcium. Many people, especially those who get little sun, need a supplement. Your doctor can advise you.
- **Protein.** Around half of bone volume is made of protein, and adequate protein supports bone strength, particularly alongside enough calcium.
- **Not smoking, and limiting alcohol.** Both speed up bone loss.
- **Fall prevention.** Balance training, good lighting at home and suitable footwear all reduce the risk of fractures.
- **Medical treatment when needed.** Hormone therapy helps prevent bone loss, and there are several effective medications for osteoporosis. A bone density scan helps your doctor decide what is right for you.

## Protecting Your Heart

Heart disease is the leading cause of death in women, and the risk rises after menopause. Before menopause, estrogen helps keep blood vessels flexible and cholesterol levels favorable. Afterward, LDL ("bad") cholesterol tends to rise, blood pressure often creeps up, and the body becomes more prone to storing fat around the abdomen, which is linked to heart disease and type 2 diabetes.

### Cholesterol, blood pressure and lifestyle

Women's heart attack symptoms can be different from the "classic" crushing chest pain. They may include breathlessness, nausea, unusual tiredness, discomfort in the jaw, neck, back or arm, or a feeling of indigestion. If you experience these, seek urgent medical help.

What helps your heart:

- **An eating pattern rich in plants**: vegetables, fruit, beans, lentils, whole grains, nuts and seeds. Mediterranean-style eating has some of the strongest evidence for heart health.
- **Fiber**, particularly soluble fiber from oats, barley, beans and lentils, which helps lower LDL cholesterol.
- **Healthy fats** such as extra virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish, in place of saturated fats from processed meats, fried foods and pastries.
- **Less salt**, mainly by cooking more at home and choosing fewer processed and takeout foods, to help manage blood pressure.
- **Regular movement**: around 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking.
- **Not smoking**, limiting alcohol, managing stress and sleeping well.

## Muscle, Balance and Healthy Aging

From around the age of 30, we gradually lose muscle mass and strength. This process, called **sarcopenia** when it becomes significant, speeds up around menopause. Muscle matters for far more than appearance. It helps you get up from a chair, carry shopping, play with grandchildren, keep your balance and stay independent. It also helps control blood sugar and supports bone health.

The two most effective ways to protect muscle are:

1. **Strength training** at least twice a week. This can be bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, free weights or machines. Part Six includes two short strength sessions a week that need no equipment.
2. **Enough protein**, spread throughout the day. Many experts now suggest that older adults aim for around 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, and possibly more if you are very active or recovering from illness, with around 25 to 30 grams at each meal. Chapter 9 shows what that looks like on your plate.

Balance naturally declines with age too, but it responds quickly to practice. Simple exercises like standing on one leg while brushing your teeth, and the balance poses in the yoga plan, can make a real difference.

## Brain Health and Staying Sharp

Many women worry about memory during the menopause transition. The brain fog of perimenopause is real, but research suggests that for most women it is temporary, with memory and concentration often improving after menopause.

Looking further ahead, women are more likely than men to develop dementia, and researchers are actively studying the role of hormones. While there is still much to learn, the habits that protect the heart also protect the brain:

- regular physical activity
- a Mediterranean-style eating pattern rich in vegetables, berries, leafy greens, beans, nuts, olive oil and fish
- good sleep
- managing blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol
- staying socially connected and mentally engaged
- protecting your hearing (hearing loss is a recognized risk factor for dementia)
- not smoking and limiting alcohol

## What Your Body Needs Most in This Stage

| Nutrient | Why it matters now | Good food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin B12** | Absorption declines with age and with some medicines (such as metformin and acid-reducing medications); low levels cause fatigue and nerve problems | Fish, meat, eggs, dairy, fortified plant milks and cereals, nutritional yeast |
| **Calcium** | Supports bone strength | Dairy, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, leafy greens |
| **Vitamin D** | Helps absorb calcium; supports muscle | Oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; often a supplement |
| **Omega-3 fats** | Support heart and brain health | Salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout, walnuts, ground flaxseed, chia seeds |
| **Protein (a little more)** | Helps protect muscle and bone with age | Spread across all meals and snacks |

Your iron needs fall after menopause, from around 18 mg to 8 mg a day, because you are no longer losing blood each month. Unless your doctor advises otherwise, most post-menopausal women do not need an iron supplement, and many multivitamins designed for women over 50 contain little or no iron for this reason.

## Know Your Numbers: The Health Checks Worth Asking For

Midlife is a great time to get a clear picture of your health. Knowing your numbers lets you and your doctor spot changes early, when they are easiest to address.

### Bone density scan, blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar

| Check | Why it matters | How often (general guide) |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood pressure** | High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms but raises the risk of heart disease and stroke | At least once a year, or as advised |
| **Cholesterol (lipid panel)** | LDL cholesterol often rises after menopause | Every few years, or more often if raised |
| **Blood sugar (HbA1c or fasting glucose)** | Detects prediabetes and diabetes early | Every few years from midlife, or as advised |
| **Bone density (DXA) scan** | Measures bone density and fracture risk | Recommended from 65, or earlier if you have risk factors |
| **Waist measurement** | A simple marker of abdominal fat and metabolic risk | Whenever you like, as a gentle check-in |
| **Vitamin D, B12, thyroid** | Common deficiencies and conditions in midlife | If you have symptoms or risk factors |
| **Cancer screening** | Breast, cervical and colorectal screening save lives | As recommended by your national screening program |

Earlier bone density testing is often advised if you went through menopause early, have a low body weight, smoke, have a parent who broke a hip, take long-term steroid medication, or have had a fracture from a minor fall. Recommendations vary between countries, so ask your doctor what is right for you.

:::summary
- Post-menopause is a new, settled stage of life. Some symptoms ease; others, like vaginal dryness, may continue and are very treatable.
- Bone loss speeds up after menopause. Strength training, calcium, vitamin D, protein and fall prevention are your best protection.
- Heart disease risk rises after menopause. Plant-rich eating, fiber, healthy fats, movement and not smoking all help.
- Muscle is one of your greatest assets for healthy aging. Protect it with strength training and enough protein.
- Vitamin B12, calcium, vitamin D, omega-3s and protein deserve particular attention, while iron needs fall.
- Know your numbers: blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar and, when appropriate, bone density.
:::
