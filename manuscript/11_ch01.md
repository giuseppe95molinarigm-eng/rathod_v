> Menopause lasts exactly one day, and almost no woman knows which day it was until a year later.

%% Renée was forty-seven when she sat in her car outside the school where she taught, trying to work out what was wrong with her. Her periods had gone from clockwork to chaos: three weeks apart, then seven, then a flood that soaked through everything. She'd started waking at two every morning. Her doctor had said "probably stress" and handed her a leaflet on sleep hygiene. Her sister said she was too young for "the change." Her best friend, a year older, swore she hadn't noticed a thing. Renée turned off the engine and typed "am I going crazy or is this menopause" into her phone. Then she deleted it, embarrassed, and went inside to teach second period.

Renée isn't going crazy. She's in the middle of one of the least explained transitions in a woman's life, and like most of us, she was never given a map.

For something that happens to half the world's population, menopause is remarkably misunderstood. Most of us grew up picturing a single moment, the day periods stop, somewhere around fifty. In reality the transition unfolds over years, sometimes more than a decade, and it often begins long before we expect. This chapter is the map. Once you know what your hormones are doing and why, everything else in this book will make more sense, and a lot of the confusing things you've been feeling will finally have an explanation.

## One Day, Many Years

Strictly speaking, **menopause is a single point in time**: the day that marks twelve months since your last period. You can only spot it looking backward. Everything before it is **perimenopause**, and everything after it is **post-menopause**. In everyday conversation, most of us use "menopause" for the whole journey, and that's fine. In this book I'll use the specific terms when the difference matters and the general one when it doesn't.

It helps to picture your reproductive life as a long road. For most of your adult years it's fairly smooth: hormones rise and fall in a predictable monthly rhythm. Perimenopause is the stretch where the road turns bumpy and unpredictable. Menopause is the signpost you pass after a full year without a period. Post-menopause is the new, smoother road beyond it. The bumpy section is where most women feel the most symptoms, and it lasts around four years on average, although for some women it's much shorter and for others it runs ten years or more.

## When It Arrives, and How

In the United States, the average age of natural menopause is 51 to 52, and most women get there somewhere between 45 and 55. Your genes play a part, so women often reach menopause at a similar age to their mothers and sisters, and smoking tends to bring it forward by a year or two.

Not every menopause arrives gradually, though. In a **natural menopause**, the ovaries slowly wind down their production of hormones over several years. A **surgical menopause** happens overnight when both ovaries are removed, and because hormone levels drop suddenly rather than gently, symptoms can be abrupt and intense. A **medically induced menopause** can follow some cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy or pelvic radiation, or medicines that deliberately quiet the ovaries, and it may be temporary or permanent.

A hysterectomy that removes the uterus but leaves the ovaries in place ends periods, but it doesn't cause menopause on its own: the ovaries keep working, although some women reach menopause a little earlier afterward. Without periods to go by, it's simply harder to tell when the transition starts, so symptoms become your main clue.

## If Your Timeline Is Different

Around 1 in 100 women reach menopause before the age of 40, which is called **premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)**. About 5 in 100 more reach it between 40 and 45, known as **early menopause**.

If that's you, your experience deserves particular care. Reaching menopause early means living longer without estrogen's protective effects on your bones, heart and brain, which is why guidelines generally recommend that women with POI or early menopause talk to a doctor about hormone therapy, usually at least until the average age of natural menopause, unless there's a medical reason not to. That's a very different conversation from the one a woman has at 55 or 60. An early or sudden menopause can also bring grief, for fertility, for the timeline you imagined, or simply for feeling "too young" for this. Those feelings are valid, and support groups for women with POI can be a lifeline.

:::note When to see a doctor sooner
If you're under 45 and your periods have become irregular or stopped, or you're having symptoms such as hot flashes and night sweats, see your doctor. Early menopause is diagnosed differently from menopause at the usual age, and it often benefits from specific treatment.
:::

## The Cast of Characters: Your Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers. They travel through your bloodstream telling different parts of your body what to do, and during the transition several of them change at once. Because they reach almost every system in the body, the effects can show up in surprising places.

**Estrogen** is the star of the show. Made mainly by the ovaries during your reproductive years, it does far more than run your cycle. It slows the rate at which old bone is broken down, keeps blood vessels flexible and cholesterol in a healthier range, helps your brain regulate temperature, supports mood, memory and sleep, keeps the skin and the tissues of the vagina and bladder plump and moist, and influences where your body stores fat. After menopause the ovaries make very little of it. Your body still produces small amounts in fat tissue and elsewhere, but levels are much lower than before.

**Progesterone** is produced after you ovulate each month. It prepares the lining of the uterus for a possible pregnancy and has a naturally calming, sleep-friendly effect on the brain. In perimenopause, ovulation becomes less regular, so progesterone is often the first hormone to fall, which can mean heavier or irregular periods, lighter sleep and feeling more on edge.

**Testosterone** isn't just a male hormone. Women make it too, in the ovaries and adrenal glands, and it contributes to libido, energy and muscle. It declines gradually from your twenties onward rather than dropping at menopause.

**FSH** (follicle-stimulating hormone) comes from the pituitary gland in your brain, and its job is to tell the ovaries to ripen an egg each month. As the ovaries become less responsive, the brain shouts louder, pumping out more FSH to try to get a reply. That's why FSH tends to rise during the transition. But in perimenopause it can swing up and down from month to month, which is why a single blood test so often tells you very little (more on that in Chapter 3).

**Cortisol** isn't a reproductive hormone, but it's always in the background. Released in response to stress, it helps regulate blood sugar, blood pressure and your sleep–wake cycle. Broken sleep, night sweats and the pressures of midlife can keep it running high, which can affect appetite, belly fat and mood. The encouraging part is that cortisol responds well to everyday habits, like regular meals, movement, rest and calming practices, and you'll find all of those in this book.

## It's the Swings, Not Just the Drop

It's tempting to imagine perimenopause as a slow, steady slide in hormones. In reality it's more like a rollercoaster. Some months, estrogen can surge higher than it was in your thirties; the next month it plunges. You might ovulate one month and not the next.

Those sharp ups and downs are behind many of the most maddening symptoms of perimenopause: periods you can't predict, sore breasts, migraines, mood swings and sleep that's fine one week and terrible the next. Many women find that once they reach post-menopause and hormones settle at a new, steady low, some of these symptoms ease, even if others, like vaginal dryness, carry on.

So if your symptoms seem to come and go with no pattern, you haven't imagined it, and you aren't doing anything wrong. Your body is adjusting to a changing landscape.

## The Three Stages at a Glance

| | Perimenopause | Menopause | Post-menopause |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The transition years before the final period | The point 12 months after your last period | All the years that follow |
| **Typical timing** | Often begins in the 40s; lasts around 4 years on average, sometimes 10 or more | Average age 51 to 52 | From menopause onward |
| **Hormones** | Fluctuating, sometimes wildly | Estrogen and progesterone now low | Settled at a new, low level |
| **Periods** | Irregular, heavier, lighter or skipped | None for 12 months | None (any bleeding needs checking) |
| **Common focus** | Cycle changes, sleep, mood, hot flashes, energy | Hot flashes, sleep, joints, intimacy, body composition | Bone, heart, muscle and brain health; lingering symptoms |

:::note Bleeding after menopause
Any vaginal bleeding after you've gone 12 months without a period should be checked by a doctor promptly. It's often caused by something harmless, but it always needs investigating.
:::

## Why Your Menopause Won't Look Like Your Friend's

Some women sail through with barely a hot flash. Others find their lives turned upside down. Most land somewhere in between. Around three in four women have hot flashes and night sweats at some point, and roughly a quarter find them severe enough to disrupt daily life.

What shapes your experience? Genes are part of it, which is why your mother's age at menopause is a reasonable clue to your own. Your health history matters too: thyroid disease, diabetes, migraines or a history of depression can all change how the transition feels. Smoking, heavy drinking, very little activity and poor sleep tend to make symptoms harder to manage, and women with a higher body weight often report more hot flashes in perimenopause. Then there's life itself. Midlife often brings children, aging parents, career pressure and relationship changes all at the same time as hormonal change. Stress doesn't cause menopause, but it can turn the volume up on everything you feel. Even attitudes matter: research suggests that the way a culture views aging and menopause can influence how women experience it.

## Culture, Ethnicity and Your Menopause

One of the largest long-term studies of women in midlife, the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), followed thousands of American women of different ethnic backgrounds for more than two decades, and it found real differences between groups. On average, Black women reported hot flashes for longer, often around ten years, than white women, while women of Japanese and Chinese heritage reported fewer hot flashes overall. Black and Hispanic women also tended to reach menopause slightly earlier.

These are averages, not predictions, and the reasons are complex: genetics, diet, stress, access to healthcare and the effects of discrimination all play a part. What matters for you is this: **your experience is valid, even if it doesn't look like what you've read about or what your friends describe.** If you feel your symptoms are being brushed off, Chapter 6 has practical tips for advocating for yourself at appointments.

Food traditions matter too. The recipes in this book draw on Mediterranean, South Asian, East Asian, Latin American and West African kitchens, because nourishing food looks different all over the world, and there's no single "right" menopause diet.
