@chapter 5 | Mind and Mood: The Emotional Side of Menopause
> What if the anxiety that arrived out of nowhere at forty-six didn't come out of nowhere at all?

%% Aisha was standing in the cereal aisle when the tears came. Nothing had happened. The store had run out of the brand her youngest liked, that was all. But suddenly she was crying behind her sunglasses, her cart half full, unable to stop. She was forty-nine, a nurse who had kept her head through twenty years of emergencies, and lately she didn't recognize herself: short-tempered with her husband, lying awake with a knot of dread she couldn't explain, forgetting words in the middle of sentences. She'd begun to wonder whether she was depressed, or worse, whether something was wrong with her brain. It didn't occur to her, not once, that it might be her hormones.

Ask women what surprised them most about perimenopause and many won't mention hot flashes. They'll talk about anxiety that came from nowhere, sudden rage over small things, crying in the supermarket, or a flatness that made it hard to enjoy anything. They'll talk about losing words mid-sentence and quietly fearing the worst.

These emotional and cognitive changes are among the most common parts of the menopause transition, and among the most often dismissed. This chapter is here to name them, explain them and point you toward help.

## Why Your Mood Changes With Your Hormones

Estrogen and progesterone have powerful effects on the brain. Estrogen influences serotonin, dopamine and other chemical messengers involved in mood, motivation and emotional resilience. Progesterone is naturally calming, partly through a substance it produces called allopregnanolone, which acts on the same brain receptors as some anti-anxiety medicines. When both hormones swing and fall, your brain has to recalibrate, and for some women that shows up as new or worsening anxiety, sometimes with a racing heart or a constant sense of dread; irritability and a much shorter fuse; low mood, tearfulness or a loss of joy; and a slipping confidence that makes once-manageable things feel overwhelming.

Research shows that the risk of depression rises during perimenopause, even in women who've never been depressed before. Women with a history of depression, postnatal depression or severe premenstrual mood symptoms (PMDD) seem to be especially sensitive to these hormonal shifts.

Hormones are only part of the story, though. Poor sleep, hot flashes, chronic stress and the sheer load of midlife all weigh on mood, and so does how we feel about aging and our changing bodies. Usually it's a mix, and that's actually good news, because it means there are several ways to feel better.

## When Big Feelings Feel Bigger

Many women describe a kind of anger in perimenopause they've never felt before: sudden, intense and out of proportion to whatever set it off. Others find themselves in tears several times a day. These moments can be frightening, and they often leave a trail of guilt.

Please be gentle with yourself here. These reactions are often a sign that your nervous system is running on empty, from hormonal shifts, sleep loss and stress all at once. In the moment, a few things can help:

- **Lengthen your out-breath.** Breathe in for four and out for six. A longer exhale switches on your body's calming response within minutes. The breathing practices in Part Six are designed for exactly this.
- **Name it.** Saying, to yourself or someone close, "this is my hormones talking, and I need a minute" can take the heat out of a situation.
- **Step away.** A short walk, a glass of cold water or a few minutes alone can let the wave pass.
- **Eat something.** Low blood sugar makes anger and tears much worse, and a snack with protein can steady things surprisingly fast.
- **Repair afterward.** Everyone loses their temper sometimes. A simple apology and explanation is enough.

If rage or tears are frequent or intense, or they're straining your relationships, talk to your doctor. Both hormonal and non-hormonal treatments can help.

## Where Did That Word Go? Brain Fog

Forgetting names, losing your train of thought, walking into a room and forgetting why, struggling to focus at work: brain fog is one of the most common and upsetting parts of the transition, and many women quietly fear early dementia, as Aisha did.

The reassuring part is that research, including findings from the SWAN study, suggests that the changes in memory and processing speed during perimenopause are generally mild and temporary, and tend to improve after menopause. Brain fog is also closely tied to poor sleep, hot flashes, stress and low mood, all of which can be treated.

So protect your sleep wherever you can (Chapter 7), keep your blood sugar steady with regular, anchored meals (Chapter 10), drink enough water, since even mild dehydration affects concentration, and keep moving, because exercise increases blood flow to the brain. Use lists, calendar reminders and a notebook by the bed without a shred of guilt; that's good strategy, not failure. And where you can, do one thing at a time, because multitasking gets harder when you're tired.

If memory problems are getting steadily worse, are affecting your ability to manage daily life, or are being noticed by others, see your doctor to rule out other causes.

## Who Am I Now?

Menopause can stir up big questions. Who am I now? What does it mean to no longer be able to have children? Why don't I recognize the woman in the mirror? Our culture often ties women's value to youth and appearance, so it's no wonder many of us feel a sense of loss. And yet many women also describe midlife as a liberation: caring less about what others think, knowing what matters, and finally having the confidence to ask for it.

Self-compassion is one of the most useful tools for this stage of life. It simply means treating yourself with the kindness you'd offer a good friend. Research links it to lower anxiety and depression, and to healthier eating and exercise habits, because we tend to look after the things we value. ==I started practicing it, awkwardly, at forty-six, by catching the voice in my head that called me "useless" when I forgot something and asking whether I'd ever say that to my daughter. I wouldn't. Slowly, the voice got quieter.==

You can start in the same way: notice your inner voice, and if you wouldn't say it to a friend, try not to say it to yourself. Talk about your body in terms of what it does for you, not only how it looks. Swap "I should" for "I'd like to." Spend time with people who make you feel good about yourself. And give some thought to what you want this next chapter of your life to hold.

## Talking About It at Home and at Work

Menopause doesn't just affect you; it touches the people around you. Partners may not understand why you're tired, irritable or less interested in sex. Children notice the shorter fuse. Colleagues may have no idea that a hot flash in a meeting is exhausting, or that you were awake half the night.

You don't have to share everything, but a simple explanation helps others understand and support you:

- *"I'm going through perimenopause. My sleep is really disrupted right now, so I'm more tired than usual."*
- *"When I get a hot flash, I just need a few minutes and some fresh air."*
- *"I'm not angry with you. My hormones are making everything feel more intense right now, and I'm working on it."*

At work, many employers now have menopause policies or support, and practical changes can make a big difference: a desk fan, access to cold water, a later start after a bad night, the option to work from home, or a more breathable uniform. If you feel comfortable, talk to your manager or HR about what would help.

## When to Reach Out for Help

Emotional symptoms deserve the same care as physical ones. Effective support is available, including hormone therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), antidepressants, counseling and peer support, and your doctor can help you choose. Please seek help promptly if you've felt persistently low, hopeless or numb for more than two weeks; if you've lost interest or pleasure in most things; if you're struggling to function at work or at home; if you're using alcohol or other substances to cope; if you're having panic attacks; or if you have thoughts of harming yourself or feel others would be better off without you.

:::note If you're in crisis
If you're having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, please reach out for help now. Call your local emergency number, go to your nearest emergency department, or contact a crisis line. In the United States, call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at any time. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans on **116 123**. You don't have to face this alone.
:::

## Reflection Prompts

Take a few quiet minutes with a notebook and consider:

1. Which emotional changes have I noticed in the past year?
2. What situations or times of day make them better or worse?
3. Who could I talk to about what I'm experiencing?
4. What's one thing I could take off my plate this month?
5. What do I want this next chapter of my life to feel like?
