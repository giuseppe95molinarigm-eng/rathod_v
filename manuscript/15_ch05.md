@chapter 5 | Mind and Mood: The Emotional Side of Menopause
> If you have felt unlike yourself, you are not imagining it, and you are certainly not alone.

Ask women what surprised them most about perimenopause and many will not mention hot flashes. They will talk about anxiety that arrived out of nowhere, sudden rage over small things, crying in the supermarket, or a flatness that made it hard to enjoy anything. They will talk about forgetting words mid-sentence and wondering whether something was seriously wrong.

These emotional and cognitive changes are among the most common, and most commonly dismissed, parts of the menopause transition. This chapter is here to validate them, explain them and point you toward help.

## Anxiety, Low Mood and Irritability: Why They Happen

Estrogen and progesterone have powerful effects on the brain. Estrogen influences serotonin, dopamine and other chemical messengers involved in mood, motivation and emotional resilience. Progesterone has a calming effect, partly through a substance it produces called allopregnanolone, which acts on the same brain receptors as some anti-anxiety medications.

When these hormones fluctuate and fall, the brain has to adjust. For some women, this shows up as:

- new or worse anxiety, sometimes with a racing heart or a constant sense of dread
- irritability and a much shorter fuse
- low mood, tearfulness or a loss of joy
- loss of confidence and self-esteem
- feeling overwhelmed by things that used to feel manageable

Research shows that the risk of depression increases during perimenopause, even in women who have never been depressed before. Women with a history of depression, postnatal depression or severe premenstrual mood symptoms (PMDD) may be more sensitive to these hormonal changes.

Hormones are only part of the story. Poor sleep, hot flashes, chronic stress and the many demands of midlife all affect mood. So does how we feel about aging and our changing bodies. It is usually a combination of factors, which is good news, because it means there are several ways to help.

## When Big Feelings Feel Bigger: Rage, Tears and Overwhelm

Many women describe a kind of anger in perimenopause they have never felt before: sudden, intense and out of proportion to whatever triggered it. Others find themselves in tears several times a day. These experiences can be frightening and can leave you feeling guilty or ashamed afterward.

Please be gentle with yourself. These reactions are often a sign that your nervous system is running on empty, from hormonal shifts, sleep deprivation and stress all at once. Some things that may help in the moment:

- **Pause and breathe.** Slow your breathing, making your out-breath longer than your in-breath (for example, in for four, out for six). This activates the body's calming response. The breathing practices in Part Six are designed for exactly these moments.
- **Name it.** Simply saying to yourself, or to someone close, "this is my hormones talking, and I need a minute" can take the heat out of a situation.
- **Step away.** A short walk, a glass of cold water or a few minutes alone can help the wave pass.
- **Eat something.** Low blood sugar can make anger and tears much worse. A snack with protein can help steady things surprisingly quickly.
- **Repair afterward.** Everyone loses their temper sometimes. A simple apology and explanation is enough.

If rage or tears are frequent, intense or affecting your relationships, please talk to your doctor. Both hormonal and non-hormonal treatments can help.

## Brain Fog and Memory Lapses

Forgetting names, losing your train of thought, walking into rooms and forgetting why, struggling to concentrate at work: brain fog is one of the most common and distressing symptoms of the transition. Many women quietly fear early dementia.

Here is the reassuring news. Research, including findings from the SWAN study, suggests that changes in memory and processing speed during perimenopause are generally mild and temporary, and that they tend to improve after menopause. Brain fog is also strongly linked to poor sleep, hot flashes, stress and low mood, all of which can be treated.

What helps:

- **Prioritize sleep** wherever you can (Chapter 7).
- **Keep blood sugar steady** with regular, balanced meals (Chapter 10).
- **Stay hydrated.** Even mild dehydration can affect concentration.
- **Move.** Exercise increases blood flow to the brain and supports memory.
- **Use external memory aids** without guilt: lists, calendar reminders, a notebook by the bed. This is not a failing; it is good strategy.
- **Do one thing at a time** where possible. Multitasking becomes harder when you are tired.

If memory problems are getting steadily worse, affect your ability to manage daily life, or are noticed by others, see your doctor to rule out other causes.

## Identity, Body Image and Self-Compassion

Menopause can stir up big questions. Who am I now? What does it mean to no longer be able to have children? Why don't I recognize the woman in the mirror? Our culture often links women's value to youth and appearance, so it is no wonder that many women feel a sense of loss during this time.

At the same time, many women describe midlife as a time of liberation: caring less about what others think, knowing what matters, and having the confidence to ask for it.

Self-compassion is one of the most useful tools for this stage of life. It means treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer a good friend. Research suggests that self-compassion is linked to lower anxiety and depression, and to healthier eating and exercise habits, because people tend to look after things they value.

A few ways to practice:

- Notice your inner voice. If you would not say it to a friend, try not to say it to yourself.
- Speak about your body in terms of what it does for you, not only how it looks.
- Replace "I should" with "I would like to" or "I'm choosing to."
- Spend time with people who make you feel good about yourself.
- Reflect on what you want this next chapter of your life to hold.

## Relationships, Work and Talking About Menopause

Menopause affects not only you but also the people around you. Partners may not understand why you are tired, irritable or less interested in sex. Children may notice your shorter fuse. Colleagues may not realize that a hot flash in a meeting is exhausting, or that you were awake half the night.

Talking about what you are experiencing can ease a lot of pressure. You don't have to share everything, but simple explanations help others understand and support you:

- *"I'm going through perimenopause. My sleep is really disrupted at the moment, so I'm more tired than usual."*
- *"When I get a hot flash, I just need a few minutes and some fresh air."*
- *"I'm not angry with you. My hormones are making everything feel more intense right now, and I'm working on it."*

At work, many employers now have menopause policies or support. Practical adjustments can make a big difference: a desk fan, access to cold water, flexible start times after a bad night, the option to work from home, or breathable uniforms. If you feel comfortable, speak to your manager or human resources team about what would help.

## When to Seek Support

Emotional symptoms deserve the same care as physical ones. Effective support is available, including hormone therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), antidepressants, counseling and peer support. Your doctor can help you decide what suits you.

### Red flags and where to turn

Please seek help promptly if you:

- feel persistently low, hopeless or numb for more than two weeks
- have lost interest or pleasure in most things
- are struggling to function at work or at home
- are using alcohol or other substances to cope
- have panic attacks
- have thoughts of harming yourself, or feel that others would be better off without you

:::note If you are in crisis
If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, please reach out for help immediately. Contact your local emergency number, go to your nearest emergency department, or call a crisis line. In the United States, you can call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at any time. In the UK and Ireland, you can call Samaritans on **116 123**. You do not have to face this alone.
:::

:::summary
- Anxiety, irritability, low mood, tearfulness and brain fog are common, real effects of the menopause transition.
- Hormonal changes affect brain chemicals involved in mood, but sleep, stress and life circumstances matter too.
- Rage and tears are often signs of an overloaded nervous system. Breathing, stepping away, eating and repairing afterward all help.
- Brain fog is usually mild and temporary, and improves with better sleep, steady blood sugar and movement.
- Self-compassion supports both mental health and healthy habits.
- Help is available. Seek support promptly if your mood is persistently low or you are having thoughts of self-harm.
:::

Take a few quiet minutes with a notebook and consider:

1. Which emotional changes have I noticed in the past year?
2. What situations or times of day make them better or worse?
3. Who could I talk to about what I am experiencing?
4. What is one thing I could take off my plate this month?
5. What do I want this next chapter of my life to feel like?
