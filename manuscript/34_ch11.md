@chapter 11 | Rethinking Weight in Midlife
> Your metabolism barely changes between the ages of 20 and 60. So why do so many women gain weight at 50?

She tried them on, and they wouldn't button. Weight is one of the most emotionally charged subjects for women in midlife. Many of us find that our weight and shape change during the transition, sometimes quickly, even though we haven't changed how we eat or move. It can feel confusing, frustrating and deeply unfair.

==I know that feeling well. In the first two years of my own transition I gained eleven pounds, almost all of it around my middle, and my first instinct was to eat less and less. It made me hungrier, crankier and, eventually, heavier.==

This chapter takes a compassionate, realistic look at what's really happening and what genuinely helps. You won't find crash diets, calorie targets or before-and-after photos here. You'll find an approach focused on health, strength and feeling good in your body, whatever its size.

## The Metabolism Myth

On average, women gain about 1.5 pounds (0.7 kg) a year in midlife, though some gain much more and some none at all. It's natural to blame a "slowing metabolism." But as you saw in Chapter 3, a landmark study of more than 6,000 people found that once you account for body size, **metabolic rate stays remarkably steady from 20 to 60.** That's liberating, because it means midlife weight change isn't a fixed sentence. It's driven by things you can influence.

So what is changing? Mostly **body composition,** the balance of muscle and fat, and **where** fat is stored.

As estrogen falls, the body shifts fat storage from the hips and thighs toward the abdomen. Muscle mass declines with age, faster around menopause, and less muscle means your body burns a little less energy and handles blood sugar less smoothly. Insulin sensitivity can dip, making it easier to store fat and harder to keep energy steady. Poor sleep raises hunger hormones, lowers fullness hormones and sends us looking for quick energy. Chronic stress and higher cortisol increase appetite and favor fat around the middle. Life changes too: busier schedules, caring responsibilities, less time to cook and move, new jobs or no job. And some medicines, including certain antidepressants and steroids, can contribute.

In other words, weight change in midlife is driven by biology and circumstance. It isn't a character flaw, and it isn't a lack of willpower.

## Why the Middle Changes Shape

Even women whose weight stays the same often notice their waistline thickening. Before menopause, estrogen encourages fat storage under the skin on the hips, buttocks and thighs (subcutaneous fat). After menopause, more fat is stored deep inside the abdomen, around the organs. This is **visceral fat.**

Visceral fat matters because it's metabolically active: it releases substances that can increase inflammation and insulin resistance, and it's linked to a higher risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes. That's why waist measurement can be a useful health check. As a general guide, many health organizations link a waist above 35 inches (88 cm) in women with higher health risks, although healthy thresholds vary with ethnicity and frame.

The encouraging news is that visceral fat responds well to the habits in this book: regular activity, especially strength training; a fiber-rich, plant-forward diet; enough protein; good sleep; less alcohol; and stress management. These can shrink visceral fat even when the scale barely moves.

## Why Crash Diets Backfire Now

When the jeans stop buttoning, the instinct is often to eat far less. Unfortunately, very low-calorie diets tend to backfire, and in midlife the cost is higher. When you eat too little, your body breaks down muscle along with fat, and losing muscle is exactly what we're trying to avoid. Low intakes of protein, calcium and vitamin D can harm your bones. Severe restriction triggers powerful biological hunger signals and cravings, and your metabolism adapts slightly to the smaller food supply. Most people regain the weight, and repeated cycles of losing and regaining can leave you with less muscle and more fat than when you started, as Gloria knows all too well. And along the way, crash diets take a toll on your mood, your energy and your relationship with food.

A slower, sustainable approach works far better in the long run.

## Fasting, Keto and Other Trends

You've probably heard intermittent fasting, keto, carb cycling and a dozen other approaches promoted for menopause.

**Intermittent fasting,** such as eating within an 8- to 10-hour window or the "5:2" pattern, helps some people eat less overall, but studies generally find it works about as well as other ways of cutting calories, not better (emerging). Some women find it easy; others find it increases hunger, irritability or poor sleep, or makes it harder to anchor each meal with enough protein. If you try it, keep your meals balanced and protein-rich.

**Ketogenic and very low-carb diets** can produce weight loss in the short term, but they're hard to sustain, often low in fiber, and can be high in saturated fat, which may raise LDL cholesterol. They also cut out beans, whole grains and fruit, foods with well-established benefits for the heart and gut.

**"Menopause supplements" and detox teas** for weight loss generally have little or no evidence behind them.

The best way of eating for you is one you can follow happily and consistently, with enough protein, fiber and nutrients, that supports your health beyond the scale. For most women, Anchor, Add, Adapt (Chapter 9) does exactly that.

If you're using or considering one of the newer weight-loss medications, see Chapter 6, which covers what they are, their side effects, and how to protect your muscle if you and your doctor choose that route.

## What Actually Works

What genuinely helps women feel healthier and more comfortable in their bodies through midlife? The research keeps pointing to the same handful of habits:

- **Anchor every meal with protein,** around 25 to 30 grams. It keeps you full, steadies blood sugar and protects muscle.
- **Add plants and fiber at every meal.** They fill you up, support your gut and help reduce visceral fat.
- **Strength train twice a week.** More muscle means better body composition, blood sugar and metabolism, even if your weight doesn't change much.
- **Walk every day,** especially after meals, to help with blood-sugar control.
- **Protect your sleep,** which regulates hunger and fullness hormones.
- **Drink less alcohol.** It adds calories, increases appetite, disrupts sleep and adds to abdominal fat.
- **Cook more, buy less ultra-processed food.** In a carefully controlled study at the US National Institutes of Health, people ate about 500 more calories a day on an ultra-processed diet than on a minimally processed one.
- **Eat with attention:** without screens, slowly, noticing your hunger and fullness.
- **Aim for "most of the time,"** not "all of the time." Changes you can keep for years beat dramatic ones that last weeks.

## Progress the Scale Can't Show

The scale tells you only one thing: your total body weight. It can't tell muscle from fat, and it swings from day to day with fluid, hormones, digestion and salt. If you're strength training, you may be building muscle and losing fat while your weight stays put.

So look for other signs. How do your clothes fit, especially around the waist? What's your waist measurement, checked once a month? Are you stronger, carrying heavier bags, climbing stairs more easily, doing more repetitions? How's your energy through the day, your sleep, your mood and confidence? Have your hot flashes, joint aches or digestion improved? What do your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar say? And how do you feel about food: calmer, more relaxed, more in tune with your body?

If you choose to weigh yourself, consider doing it no more than once a week, at the same time of day, and focus on the trend over several weeks rather than any single number. If weighing yourself causes distress, it's perfectly fine not to.
