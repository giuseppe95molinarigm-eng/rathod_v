@chapter 11 | Rethinking Weight in Midlife
> Your body is not failing you. It is changing, and it deserves understanding, not punishment.

Weight is one of the most emotionally charged topics for women in midlife. Many women find that their weight and shape change during the menopause transition, sometimes quite quickly, even though they haven't changed how they eat or move. It can feel confusing, frustrating and unfair.

This chapter takes a compassionate and realistic look at what is happening and what genuinely helps. You won't find crash diets, calorie targets or before-and-after photos here. You will find an approach focused on health, strength and feeling good in your body, whatever its size.

## Why Weight and Shape Change During Menopause

On average, women gain around 1.5 pounds (0.7 kg) a year during midlife, although some gain much more and some none at all. Perhaps even more noticeable than the number on the scale is the change in **body composition** (the balance of muscle and fat) and **where** fat is stored.

### Hormones, muscle loss, sleep, stress: it's not a lack of willpower

Several factors work together:

- **Falling estrogen** changes where the body stores fat, shifting it from the hips and thighs toward the abdomen.
- **Muscle loss.** Muscle mass declines with age, and the process tends to accelerate around menopause. Less muscle means your body burns a little less energy, and it can also affect blood-sugar control.
- **Changes in insulin sensitivity** can make it easier to store fat and harder to keep energy steady.
- **Poor sleep** increases hunger hormones, reduces fullness hormones and makes us more likely to reach for quick energy.
- **Stress** and higher cortisol levels can increase appetite and favor fat storage around the middle.
- **Life changes.** Busy schedules, caring responsibilities, less time to cook and exercise, and changes in work all play a part.
- **Medications** such as some antidepressants and steroids can contribute to weight gain.

In other words, weight change in midlife is driven by biology and life circumstances. It is not a character flaw.

## Why Your Shape May Shift to the Middle

Even women whose weight stays the same often notice their waistline thickening. Before menopause, estrogen encourages the body to store fat under the skin on the hips, buttocks and thighs (subcutaneous fat). After menopause, more fat tends to be stored deep within the abdomen, around the organs. This is called **visceral fat**.

Visceral fat matters because it is metabolically active: it releases substances that can increase inflammation and insulin resistance, and it is linked with a higher risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes. This is why waist measurement can be a useful health check. As a general guide, many health organizations suggest that a waist measurement above 35 inches (88 cm) for women is linked to increased health risks, although healthy thresholds vary by ethnicity and body frame.

The encouraging news is that visceral fat responds well to the habits in this book: regular physical activity (including strength training), a fiber-rich, plant-forward eating pattern, enough protein, good sleep, reduced alcohol and stress management. These habits can reduce visceral fat even when the scale doesn't change much.

## Why Crash Diets Backfire, Especially Now

When women notice weight gain, the instinct is often to eat much less. Unfortunately, very low-calorie diets tend to backfire, and in midlife the costs can be higher:

- **They cause muscle loss.** When you eat too little, your body breaks down muscle as well as fat. Losing muscle is exactly what we want to avoid during and after menopause.
- **They can harm bone health,** particularly if calcium, vitamin D and protein intakes are low.
- **They increase hunger and cravings.** Severe restriction triggers powerful biological responses that drive you to eat.
- **They slow your metabolism** slightly as your body adapts to less food.
- **They are rarely sustainable.** Most people regain weight after a crash diet, and repeated cycles of losing and regaining can leave you with less muscle and more fat than before.
- **They take a toll on your relationship with food,** your mood and your energy.

A gentler, sustainable approach is far more effective in the long run.

## Fasting, Keto and Other Trends: What the Evidence Really Says

You have probably heard about intermittent fasting, keto, carb cycling and dozens of other approaches promoted for menopause. Here is a balanced look at the most popular ones:

- **Intermittent fasting** (such as eating within an 8- to 10-hour window, or "5:2" fasting) can help some people eat less overall. However, studies generally find it works about as well as other ways of reducing calories, not better (emerging). Some women find it helpful and easy; others find it increases hunger, irritability or poor sleep, or makes it harder to get enough protein. If you try it, make sure your meals are still balanced and protein-rich.
- **Ketogenic (keto) and very low-carb diets** can lead to weight loss in the short term, but they are difficult to sustain, often low in fiber, and may be high in saturated fat, which can raise LDL cholesterol. They also limit beans, whole grains and fruit, which have well-established benefits for heart and gut health.
- **"Menopause supplements" and detox teas** for weight loss generally have little or no evidence behind them.

The best eating pattern for you is one you can follow happily and consistently, that includes enough protein, fiber and nutrients, and that supports your health beyond the scale. For most women, the Menopause Plate (Chapter 9) offers exactly that.

## GLP-1 Medications and Menopause: Eating to Protect Your Muscle

GLP-1 medications, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, have become widely used for weight management and type 2 diabetes. They work by mimicking hormones that regulate appetite and blood sugar, helping people feel full sooner and for longer. For many people, they lead to significant weight loss and health improvements.

If you are using, or considering, one of these medications, there are a few important things to know:

- **Muscle loss is a concern.** In clinical trials, a substantial share of the weight lost on these medications (often a quarter or more) was lean mass, which includes muscle. For midlife women, who are already losing muscle more quickly, protecting it is essential.
- **Protein becomes even more important.** Because appetite is reduced, it can be hard to eat enough. Prioritize protein at every meal, aiming for around 25 to 30 grams, and eat your protein first.
- **Strength training is essential.** Resistance exercise at least twice a week helps maintain muscle and bone while you lose weight.
- **Nutrient density matters.** With smaller portions, choose foods that pack in nutrients: vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, dairy or fortified alternatives, fish and lean protein.
- **Stay hydrated** and increase fiber gradually to help manage common side effects such as nausea and constipation.
- **Work with your healthcare team,** including a dietitian if possible, and discuss how the medication may interact with other treatments.

## Sustainable Strategies That Work

What genuinely helps women feel healthier and more comfortable in their bodies through midlife? The research consistently points to a handful of habits.

### Protein, fiber, strength, sleep, gentle consistency

- **Protein at every meal.** Protein keeps you full, steadies blood sugar and protects muscle. Aim for around 25 to 30 grams per meal.
- **Fiber at every meal.** Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans and lentils help you feel full, support gut health and reduce visceral fat.
- **Strength training twice a week.** Building muscle improves body composition, blood sugar and metabolism, even if your weight doesn't change much.
- **Daily movement.** Walking, especially after meals, supports blood-sugar control and overall health.
- **Sleep.** Protecting sleep helps regulate hunger and fullness hormones and makes it easier to make choices you feel good about.
- **Less alcohol.** Alcohol adds calories, increases appetite, disrupts sleep and contributes to abdominal fat.
- **Fewer ultra-processed foods.** Foods that are highly processed tend to be easy to overeat. A study at the US National Institutes of Health found that people ate around 500 more calories a day on an ultra-processed diet than on a minimally processed one.
- **Mindful eating.** Eating without distractions, slowly, and paying attention to your hunger and fullness helps you enjoy food more and recognize when you have had enough.
- **Gentle consistency.** Small changes that you can maintain for years beat dramatic changes that last weeks. Aim for "most of the time," not "all of the time."

## Measuring Progress Beyond the Scale

The scale tells you only one thing: your total body weight. It can't distinguish between muscle and fat, and it fluctuates from day to day with fluid, hormones, digestion and salt. If you are strength training, you may build muscle and lose fat while your weight stays the same.

Instead of relying on the scale alone, try noticing:

- **How your clothes fit,** particularly around the waist
- **Your waist measurement,** checked once a month
- **Your strength:** can you carry heavier shopping, climb stairs more easily, or do more repetitions of an exercise?
- **Your energy** through the day
- **Your sleep quality**
- **Your mood and confidence**
- **Your symptoms,** such as hot flashes, joint aches or digestion
- **Your health markers:** blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar
- **How you feel about food:** calmer, more relaxed and more in tune with your body

If you choose to weigh yourself, consider doing it no more than once a week, at the same time of day, and focus on the overall trend over several weeks rather than any single number. If weighing yourself causes distress, it is perfectly fine not to.
