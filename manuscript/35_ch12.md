%% Fatima used to dread Sunday evenings, when the week ahead loomed and the fridge held half an onion and a jar of olives. At forty-seven, with two jobs' worth of work squeezed into one, a son who ate like a linebacker and a husband who worked late, dinner had become a nightly scramble that usually ended in takeout. Then a friend talked her into trying "the Sunday hour." Now, while the kettle boils for her tea, she puts two trays of vegetables in the oven, starts a pot of lentils and boils a dozen eggs. By the time her tea's gone cold, the week is half cooked. "I didn't get more time," she says. "I just stopped wasting the time I had."

You now know what your body needs. This chapter is about making it happen on real weekdays, when you're tired, short on time and juggling a dozen other things. The goal isn't to spend more time in the kitchen. It's to spend the time you have more wisely, the way Fatima does.

## Twenty Minutes to a Plan

A weekly plan is one of the most powerful tools for eating well. It saves time, money and stress, and it means you're far less likely to end up eating whatever's fastest when you're exhausted. This routine takes about 20 minutes:

1. **Look at your week (2 minutes).** Which evenings are busy? When will you have time to cook? Any nights out?
2. **Pick your dinners (5 minutes).** Choose three or four main recipes, including at least one batch-cook or slow-cooker meal that makes leftovers. Plan one "pantry night" with something quick, and leave one night flexible.
3. **Turn leftovers into lunches (3 minutes).** Most dinners in this book become the next day's lunch, as they are or with a simple twist.
4. **Choose two breakfasts (3 minutes):** one make-ahead (like overnight oats or egg muffins) and one quick option for variety.
5. **Pick two or three snacks (2 minutes).**
6. **Write your grocery list (5 minutes).** Check what you already have, then list what you need by store section to save time in the aisles.

Part Five includes a ready-made 12-week meal plan with grocery lists, so you can skip the planning altogether if you prefer, and Appendix B has a blank planner for weeks when you want to make your own.

## Cook Once, Eat Three Times

This is the secret to eating well without cooking every night: one batch of food becomes three different meals.

| Cook once | Eat it again | And again |
|---|---|---|
| Sheet-Pan Salmon with Broccoli & Sweet Potato (dinner) | Flaked into a grain bowl with leafy greens (lunch) | Mashed with Greek yogurt and herbs on rye crackers (snack or light lunch) |
| Three-Bean Vegetable Chili (dinner) | Spooned over a baked sweet potato (lunch) | Wrapped in a whole-grain tortilla with avocado (quick dinner) |
| Lemon Herb Chicken Grain Bowl (lunch) | Extra chicken in a wrap with hummus and salad (lunch) | Stirred into soup with white beans and kale (dinner) |
| Spiced Red Lentil Dal with Spinach (dinner) | With brown rice and yogurt (lunch) | Thinned with stock into a warming soup (lunch) |

## Your Grocery List, Aisle by Aisle

A well-stocked kitchen makes the healthy choice the easy one. Use this list as a template and adapt it to your tastes, budget and needs.

**Produce**

- Leafy greens: spinach, kale, arugula, mixed salad leaves, bok choy
- Cruciferous vegetables: broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts
- Colorful vegetables: bell peppers, carrots, tomatoes, beets, zucchini, red onions
- Aromatics: garlic, ginger, onions, scallions, fresh herbs, lemons, limes
- Starchy vegetables: sweet potatoes, potatoes, squash, corn
- Fruit: berries, apples, pears, oranges, bananas, kiwi
- Avocados

**Protein**

- Fish: salmon, cod, trout; canned sardines, salmon and tuna
- Poultry: chicken breasts or thighs, lean ground turkey
- Eggs
- Tofu (calcium-set if possible), tempeh, edamame
- Lean red meat (now and then, if you eat it)

**Dairy and alternatives**

- Plain Greek yogurt, kefir, cottage cheese
- Milk, or fortified unsweetened soy milk (good for calcium and protein)
- Cheese, such as feta, Parmesan and ricotta, in moderate amounts

**Pantry**

- Whole grains: rolled oats, quinoa, brown rice, whole-grain pasta, barley, buckwheat
- Whole-grain or seeded rye bread, whole-grain tortillas, corn tortillas
- Canned beans, chickpeas and lentils; dried red lentils
- Canned tomatoes, tomato paste, low-sodium stock
- Nuts and seeds: almonds, walnuts, pumpkin seeds, chia seeds, ground flaxseed, sesame seeds

- Extra virgin olive oil, toasted sesame oil

- Spices: turmeric, cumin, cinnamon, paprika, chili flakes, garam masala, dried oregano

**Freezer**

- Frozen vegetables: spinach, peas, broccoli, stir-fry mixes, cauliflower rice

- Frozen edamame
- Frozen fish fillets and shrimp
- Batch-cooked soups, stews, chili and dal

## Dinner From an "Empty" Fridge

With a few basics in the cupboard, you can make a real meal in minutes even when the fridge looks bare. A **bean and greens skillet** comes together from canned white beans, frozen spinach, garlic, canned tomatoes and a drizzle of olive oil, topped with an egg or served with toast. **Speedy fried rice** uses frozen brown rice, frozen vegetables, eggs or tofu, tamari and sesame oil. **Pantry pasta** is whole-grain pasta with canned tuna or chickpeas, canned tomatoes, olives, capers and frozen peas. For a **loaded sweet potato,** microwave one and pile on canned black beans, salsa, Greek yogurt and avocado. And a carton of good vegetable soup becomes a meal with a can of lentils and a handful of frozen spinach stirred in.

## Add First, Swap Second

Small changes add up. Instead of thinking about what to cut out, think about what you can add, and which swaps feel easy.

| Instead of... | Try... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| White bread | Seeded rye or whole-grain bread | More fiber, steadier energy |
| Sugary breakfast cereal | Overnight oats with berries and seeds | Protein, fiber and longer-lasting fullness |
| Flavored, sweetened yogurt | Plain Greek yogurt with fruit and cinnamon | More protein, less added sugar |
| Chips | Crispy Roasted Chickpeas or a small handful of nuts | Protein, fiber, healthy fats |
| Sweetened soda | Sparkling water with citrus, berries or cucumber | No added sugar; helps hydration |
| Creamy salad dressing | Olive oil, lemon and herbs | Heart-healthy fats |
| White rice | Brown rice, quinoa, or half rice and half cauliflower rice | More fiber and nutrients |
| Processed deli meats | Leftover roast chicken, canned salmon, hummus or egg | Less salt and processed meat |
| Evening glass of wine | Tart Cherry Evening Spritz or an alcohol-free alternative | Better sleep, fewer hot flashes |
| Afternoon cookie | Two squares of dark chocolate with almonds | Satisfies the sweet craving with added nutrients |
| Cream in coffee | Milk or fortified soy milk | Less saturated fat, more calcium |

And the easy additions: a handful of spinach in eggs, sauces and smoothies; a spoonful of ground flaxseed on oats or yogurt; a can of beans in soups and stews; seeds on salads; an extra vegetable with dinner; a piece of fruit with lunch.

## The Sunday Hour

A little preparation at the start of the week makes weekday eating far easier. You don't need a whole afternoon; 60 to 90 minutes on a Sunday, or whatever day suits you, is plenty. Each week of the meal plan in Part Five includes a prep plan, and this is the basic routine:

1. **Turn on the oven** and roast two trays of vegetables (peppers, zucchini, red onion, broccoli, sweet potato) with olive oil.
2. **Cook a pot of grains,** such as quinoa or brown rice, for the next three or four days.
3. **Prepare a protein:** bake chicken, make a tray of Spinach & Feta Egg Muffins, or marinate a block of tofu.
4. **Make one batch meal,** such as a soup, chili or dal, for lunches or a busy evening.
5. **Wash and chop** salad vegetables and store them in containers lined with paper towels.
6. **Make overnight oats** or chia pudding for two or three breakfasts.
7. **Shake up a jar of salad dressing.**
8. **Portion snacks,** like nuts, seeds or energy bites, into small containers.

### Keeping it safe

Cool cooked food quickly and refrigerate it within two hours. Most cooked dishes keep for three to four days in the fridge, and most soups, stews and chilis keep for about three months in the freezer, so label and date everything. Reheat food until it's steaming hot all the way through, reheat rice only once, and defrost frozen food in the fridge overnight rather than on the counter.

## Drinking Enough Without Thinking About It

Many of us find it hard to drink enough, especially on busy days. Start with a glass of water before your morning coffee or tea. Keep a reusable bottle with you and refill it through the day. Link drinking to habits you already have: a glass with every meal, after every bathroom trip, or every time you check your email. Make water more interesting with citrus, cucumber, mint, berries or ginger. Herbal tea, milk, soy milk, sparkling water, and even tea and coffee all count, and so do water-rich foods like soup, cucumber, watermelon, oranges, berries and tomatoes. Drink extra after exercise, in hot weather and after hot flashes or night sweats.

## Restaurants, Travel and Celebrations

Healthy eating should fit around your life, not the other way round. Social meals, celebrations and travel are part of a happy, connected life, and a few habits help you enjoy them and still feel good. Wherever you are, look for your anchor and your plants: a protein, plenty of vegetables and a fiber-rich carb, which nearly every menu offers. Don't arrive starving; a small snack beforehand makes choosing easier. Ask for what you need, like dressing on the side, extra vegetables or grilled instead of fried. Choose your treats: enjoy the dish or dessert you really want, slowly, and skip the ones you don't care about. Pace alcohol by alternating with water or trying alcohol-free options. When you travel, pack nuts, fruit, roasted chickpeas or protein bars, and look for a grocery store for easy meals. And let go of guilt. One meal or one weekend won't undo anything. Enjoy it, and pick up your usual routine at the next meal.

## One Meal, Whole Family

You don't need to cook one meal for yourself and another for everyone else. The recipes in this book are family-friendly, and a few tricks help everyone enjoy the same dinner. Build-your-own meals like tacos, fajita bowls, grain bowls and stir-fries let each person choose their own toppings and portions. Adjust portions rather than recipes: growing teenagers or very active family members can take more grains and starchy vegetables while you prioritize your anchor and your plants. Put chili, hot sauce and strong spices on the table instead of in the pot, for those who like them (and for those whose hot flashes don't). Serve vegetables first, as a starter or side, when everyone's hungriest. And get the family involved, because children and partners who help choose and cook meals are far more likely to eat them.

## Eating Well on a Budget

Nourishing food doesn't have to be expensive, and some of the healthiest foods are among the cheapest. Beans, lentils and chickpeas, canned or dried, are inexpensive protein and fiber, and dried are cheaper still. Frozen fruit and vegetables are just as nutritious as fresh, last for months and cut waste. Canned sardines, salmon and tuna provide protein and omega-3s, plus calcium if you eat the bones. Eggs and tofu are affordable, versatile anchors, and oats, brown rice and whole-grain pasta are cheap staples. Buy in season, choose store brands, and buy whole rather than prepared: a whole chicken, a bag of carrots or a block of cheese costs less than the pre-cut versions. Above all, plan and use your leftovers, because food waste is one of the biggest hidden costs in most households, and batch cooking and freezing portions helps with that too.

## Making Any Recipe Work for You

Every recipe in this book includes labels and swaps so you can adapt it. These general swaps work across most of them:

| If you need... | Swap... | For... |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluten-free** | Wheat pasta, bread, couscous, tortillas | Gluten-free pasta and bread, quinoa, rice, buckwheat, corn tortillas |
| **Gluten-free** | Soy sauce | Tamari or coconut aminos |
| **Gluten-free** | Rolled oats | Certified gluten-free oats |
| **Dairy-free** | Milk | Fortified unsweetened soy, oat or almond milk |
| **Dairy-free** | Yogurt | Unsweetened soy or coconut yogurt (check for added calcium) |
| **Dairy-free** | Feta or Parmesan | Dairy-free alternatives, or nutritional yeast for a savory flavor |
| **Vegetarian** | Chicken, turkey or fish | Tofu, tempeh, beans, lentils or eggs |
| **Vegan** | Eggs (in baking) | 1 tbsp ground flaxseed mixed with 3 tbsp water per egg |
| **Vegan** | Honey | Maple syrup or date syrup |
| **Vegan** | Fish sauce | Soy sauce or miso with a squeeze of lime |

If you follow a vegan diet, pay particular attention to vitamin B12 (a supplement is essential), calcium, vitamin D, iron, iodine and omega-3s, and mix your protein sources every day. A registered dietitian can help you plan a balanced vegan diet.
