@chapter 12 | The Busy Woman's Kitchen
> Healthy eating doesn't have to take hours. It just needs a little planning and a few smart habits.

You now know what your body needs. This chapter is about making it happen on real weekdays, when you are tired, short on time and juggling a dozen other things. The goal is not to spend more time in the kitchen. It is to spend the time you have more wisely.

## Meal Planning in 20 Minutes a Week

A simple weekly plan is one of the most powerful tools for eating well. It saves time, money and stress, and it means you are much less likely to end up eating whatever is quickest when you are exhausted. Here is a 20-minute routine:

1. **Check your week (2 minutes).** Look at your calendar. Which evenings are busy? When will you have time to cook? Are there any social events or nights out?
2. **Choose your dinners (5 minutes).** Pick three or four main recipes for the week, including at least one batch-cook or slow-cooker meal that makes leftovers. Plan one "pantry night" with something quick, and leave room for one flexible night.
3. **Plan lunches from leftovers (3 minutes).** Most dinners in this book can become the next day's lunch, either as they are or with a simple twist.
4. **Pick two breakfasts (3 minutes).** Choose one make-ahead option (such as overnight oats or egg muffins) and one quick option for variety.
5. **Choose two or three snacks (2 minutes).**
6. **Write your grocery list (5 minutes).** Check what you already have in the fridge, freezer and pantry, then list what you need by store section to save time.

Part Five includes a ready-made 12-week meal plan with grocery lists, so you can skip the planning altogether if you prefer. Appendix B has a blank weekly planner for when you want to create your own.

### Cook once, eat three times

This is the secret to eating well without spending every evening cooking. One batch of food becomes three different meals:

| Cook once | Eat it again | And again |
|---|---|---|
| Sheet-Pan Salmon with Broccoli & Sweet Potato (dinner) | Flaked into a grain bowl with leafy greens (lunch) | Mashed with Greek yogurt and herbs on rye crackers (snack or light lunch) |
| Three-Bean Vegetable Chili (dinner) | Spooned over a baked sweet potato (lunch) | Wrapped in a whole-grain tortilla with avocado (quick dinner) |
| Lemon Herb Chicken Grain Bowl (lunch) | Extra chicken in a wrap with hummus and salad (lunch) | Stirred into soup with white beans and kale (dinner) |
| Spiced Red Lentil Dal with Spinach (dinner) | With brown rice and yogurt (lunch) | Thinned with stock as a warming soup (lunch) |

## The Menopause-Friendly Grocery Guide

A well-stocked kitchen makes healthy choices the easy choices. Use this list as a template and adapt it to your tastes, budget and dietary needs.

### Printable list by store section: produce, protein, pantry, dairy and alternatives, freezer

**Produce**

- Leafy greens: spinach, kale, arugula, mixed salad leaves, bok choy
- Cruciferous vegetables: broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts
- Colorful vegetables: bell peppers, carrots, tomatoes, beets, zucchini, red onions
- Aromatics: garlic, ginger, onions, scallions, fresh herbs, lemons, limes
- Starchy vegetables: sweet potatoes, potatoes, squash, corn
- Fruit: berries, apples, pears, oranges, bananas, kiwi
- Avocados

**Protein**

- Fish: salmon, cod, trout; canned sardines, salmon and tuna
- Poultry: chicken breasts or thighs, lean ground turkey
- Eggs
- Tofu (calcium-set if possible), tempeh, edamame
- Lean red meat (occasionally, if you eat it)

**Dairy and alternatives**

- Plain Greek yogurt, kefir, cottage cheese
- Milk, or fortified unsweetened soy milk (good for calcium and protein)
- Cheese, such as feta, Parmesan and ricotta, in moderate amounts

**Pantry**

- Whole grains: rolled oats, quinoa, brown rice, whole-grain pasta, barley, buckwheat
- Whole-grain or seeded rye bread, whole-grain tortillas, corn tortillas
- Canned beans, chickpeas and lentils; dried red lentils
- Canned tomatoes, tomato paste, low-sodium stock
- Nuts and seeds: almonds, walnuts, pumpkin seeds, chia seeds, ground flaxseed, sesame seeds
- Nut and seed butters (no added sugar)
- Extra virgin olive oil, sesame oil
- Vinegars, low-sodium soy sauce or tamari, miso paste, Dijon mustard
- Spices: turmeric, cumin, cinnamon, paprika, chili flakes, garam masala, dried oregano
- Dark chocolate (70% or higher)

**Freezer**

- Frozen vegetables: spinach, peas, broccoli, mixed stir-fry vegetables, cauliflower rice
- Frozen berries and mango
- Frozen edamame
- Frozen fish fillets and shrimp
- Batch-cooked soups, stews, chili and dal
- Whole-grain bread (slices toast perfectly from frozen)

## Pantry, Fridge and Freezer Staples

With a few basics on hand, you can make a healthy meal in minutes, even when the fridge looks empty. Some "pantry night" ideas:

- **Bean and greens skillet:** canned white beans, frozen spinach, garlic, canned tomatoes and a drizzle of olive oil, topped with an egg or served with toast.
- **Speedy fried rice:** frozen brown rice, frozen vegetables, eggs or tofu, tamari and sesame oil.
- **Pantry pasta:** whole-grain pasta, canned tuna or chickpeas, canned tomatoes, olives, capers and frozen peas.
- **Loaded sweet potato:** microwave a sweet potato and top with canned black beans, salsa, Greek yogurt and avocado.
- **Five-minute soup upgrade:** a carton of good-quality vegetable soup with added canned lentils and a handful of frozen spinach.

## Easy Additions and Swaps

Small changes add up. Instead of focusing on what to cut out, think about what you can add, and which simple swaps feel easy.

### Simple swap table, e.g., white bread to seeded rye

| Instead of... | Try... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| White bread | Seeded rye or whole-grain bread | More fiber, steadier energy |
| Sugary breakfast cereal | Overnight oats with berries and seeds | Protein, fiber and longer-lasting fullness |
| Flavored, sweetened yogurt | Plain Greek yogurt with fruit and cinnamon | More protein, less added sugar |
| Chips or crisps | Crispy Roasted Chickpeas or a small handful of nuts | Protein, fiber, healthy fats |
| Sweetened soda | Sparkling water with citrus, berries or cucumber | No added sugar; helps hydration |
| Creamy salad dressing | Olive oil, lemon and herbs | Heart-healthy fats |
| White rice | Brown rice, quinoa or half rice and half cauliflower rice | More fiber and nutrients |
| Processed deli meats | Leftover roast chicken, canned salmon, hummus or egg | Less salt and processed meat |
| Evening glass of wine | Tart Cherry Evening Spritz or an alcohol-free alternative | Better sleep, fewer hot flashes |
| Afternoon cookie | Two squares of dark chocolate with almonds | Satisfies sweet cravings with added nutrients |
| Cream in coffee | Milk or fortified soy milk | Less saturated fat, more calcium |

**Easy additions:** a handful of spinach in eggs, sauces and smoothies; a tablespoon of ground flaxseed on oats or yogurt; a can of beans in soups and stews; seeds sprinkled on salads; an extra vegetable with every dinner; a piece of fruit with lunch.

## Batch Cooking and Prep-Ahead Routines

A little preparation at the start of the week makes weekday eating much easier. You don't need a whole afternoon: 60 to 90 minutes on a Sunday (or any day that works for you) is enough. Each week of the meal plan in Part Five includes a prep plan, but here is the basic routine:

### A 60-minute prep session

1. **Turn on the oven** and roast two trays of vegetables (for example, peppers, zucchini, red onion, broccoli and sweet potato) with olive oil.
2. **Cook a pot of grains,** such as quinoa or brown rice, for the next three or four days.
3. **Prepare a protein,** such as baking chicken, a tray of Spinach & Feta Egg Muffins or a block of marinated tofu.
4. **Make one batch meal,** such as a soup, chili or dal, for lunches or a busy evening.
5. **Wash and chop** salad vegetables and store them in containers lined with paper towels.
6. **Make overnight oats** or chia pudding for two or three breakfasts.
7. **Mix a jar of salad dressing.**
8. **Portion snacks,** such as nuts, seeds or energy bites, into small containers.

### Food storage and safety

- Cool cooked food quickly and refrigerate within two hours.
- Most cooked dishes keep for three to four days in the fridge.
- Label and date food in the freezer. Most soups, stews and chilis keep for around three months.
- Reheat food until it is steaming hot all the way through, and reheat rice only once.
- Defrost frozen food in the fridge overnight, not on the counter.

## Hydration Made Easy

Many women find it hard to drink enough, especially when they are busy. A few simple habits help:

- Drink a glass of water when you wake up, before your morning coffee or tea.
- Keep a reusable water bottle with you and refill it throughout the day.
- Link drinking to daily habits: a glass with every meal, after every bathroom trip, or every time you check your email.
- Make water more interesting with slices of citrus, cucumber, mint, berries or ginger.
- Herbal teas, milk, soy milk, sparkling water, and even tea and coffee all count toward your fluid intake.
- Eat water-rich foods, such as soups, cucumber, watermelon, oranges, berries and tomatoes.
- Drink extra after exercise, in hot weather and after hot flashes or night sweats.

## Eating Out, Travel and Social Occasions

Healthy eating should fit around your life, not the other way round. Social meals, celebrations and travel are important parts of a happy, connected life. A few tips help you enjoy them while still feeling good:

- **Look for the plate.** Wherever you are, look for a protein, plenty of vegetables and a fiber-rich carb. Most menus have something that fits.
- **Don't arrive starving.** A small snack beforehand helps you make choices you feel good about.
- **Ask for what you need.** Dressing on the side, extra vegetables, or grilled instead of fried are reasonable requests.
- **Choose your treats.** Enjoy the dish or dessert you really want, slowly, and skip the ones you don't care about.
- **Pace alcohol.** Alternate each drink with a glass of water, or try alcohol-free options.
- **When traveling,** pack snacks such as nuts, fruit, roasted chickpeas and protein bars, and look for grocery stores for simple meals.
- **Let go of guilt.** One meal or one weekend won't undo your progress. Enjoy it, and return to your usual routine at your next meal.

## One Meal, Whole Family: Cooking for Everyone at Once

You do not need to cook separate meals for yourself and your family. The recipes in this book are designed to be family-friendly. A few tips help everyone enjoy the same meal:

- **Build-your-own meals** work brilliantly: tacos, fajita bowls, grain bowls and stir-fries let everyone choose their own toppings and portions.
- **Adjust portions, not recipes.** Growing teenagers or very active family members can have larger portions of grains and starchy vegetables, while you prioritize protein and vegetables.
- **Add spice at the table.** Keep chili, hot sauce and strong spices on the side for those who like them (and for those who find spicy food triggers hot flashes).
- **Serve vegetables first,** as a starter or side, when everyone is hungry.
- **Involve the family.** Children and partners who help choose and cook meals are much more likely to eat them.

## Eating Well on a Budget

Nutritious food doesn't have to be expensive. Some of the healthiest foods are also among the cheapest:

- **Beans, lentils and chickpeas,** canned or dried, are inexpensive sources of protein and fiber. Dried versions are even cheaper.
- **Frozen fruit and vegetables** are just as nutritious as fresh, last for months and reduce waste.
- **Canned fish,** such as sardines, salmon and tuna, provides protein, omega-3s and, with bones, calcium.
- **Eggs and tofu** are affordable, versatile proteins.
- **Oats, brown rice and whole-grain pasta** are inexpensive staples.
- **Buy in season,** and choose store brands.
- **Plan and use leftovers** to reduce food waste, which is one of the biggest hidden costs in most households.
- **Cook in batches** and freeze portions.
- **Buy whole and prepare yourself:** a whole chicken, a bag of carrots or a block of cheese costs less than pre-cut versions.

## Adapting Recipes: Gluten-Free, Dairy-Free, Vegetarian and Vegan

Every recipe in this book includes labels and swaps, so you can adapt it to your needs. These general swaps work across most recipes:

| If you need... | Swap... | For... |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluten-free** | Wheat pasta, bread, couscous, tortillas | Gluten-free pasta, bread, quinoa, rice, buckwheat, corn tortillas |
| **Gluten-free** | Soy sauce | Tamari or coconut aminos |
| **Gluten-free** | Rolled oats | Certified gluten-free oats |
| **Dairy-free** | Milk | Fortified unsweetened soy, oat or almond milk |
| **Dairy-free** | Yogurt | Unsweetened soy or coconut yogurt (check for added calcium) |
| **Dairy-free** | Feta or Parmesan | Dairy-free alternatives, or nutritional yeast for a savory flavor |
| **Vegetarian** | Chicken, turkey or fish | Tofu, tempeh, beans, lentils or eggs |
| **Vegan** | Eggs (in baking) | 1 tbsp ground flaxseed mixed with 3 tbsp water per egg |
| **Vegan** | Honey | Maple syrup or date syrup |
| **Vegan** | Fish sauce | Soy sauce or miso with a squeeze of lime |

If you follow a vegan diet, pay particular attention to vitamin B12 (a supplement is essential), calcium, vitamin D, iron, iodine and omega-3s, and include a variety of protein sources every day. A registered dietitian can help you plan a balanced vegan diet.
